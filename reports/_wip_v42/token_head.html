Token and fee

The first two versions of this spec forbade a protocol fee and a token. On 10 November 2026 the owner decided to add both, to keep the pool fully immutable, and to keep the project decentralized. This section gives them the only shape that fits those three decisions, and says plainly what they change.

The fee

  • 0.2% on the way in and 0.2% on the way out, taken in the asset itself. Together that is 0.4%, against Railgun's 0.5%. Transfers inside the pool are free.
  • The entry fee is paid on top of the deposit and taken when the deposit is activated, so a refunded deposit is returned with its fee. The exit fee comes out of the standard size.
  • The rate and the destination are fixed in code. Nobody can raise, lower or redirect it.
  • It goes to an immutable distributor: 40% to token stakers in proportion to stake, 40% split equally among the screeners who revealed that day's accepted batch, and 20% to buy the token from its locked pool.
  • Stakers are paid in the asset the fee was taken in, evenly over the seven days after each sweep, so staking for one block around a sweep earns nothing extra. A later sweep spreads what is left together with the new amount.
  • When the registry has accepted no batch for a week, and for good once deposits have closed, the screeners' 40% goes to stakers.

The public pool

  • The launch transaction creates the token, then places 25% of supply as one-sided liquidity in a Uniswap v3 pool against IMD, at the 1% fee tier. Buyers bring IMD and the price rises as they buy. There is no ETH pair.
  • The range starts at a valuation of 48,000 IMD for the whole supply, about $127,000 with IMD at $2.64, and runs upward without a practical limit so the pool never runs out of tokens to buy. With a share f of the pool's tokens bought, the pool holds 12,000 × f / (1 − f) IMD and the price is the start divided by (1 − f)². At half bought that is 12,000 IMD and four times the start. The simulation tabulates it.
  • A one-sided pool starts with no IMD in it, so sellers can only sell into what buyers have paid, and below the starting price there are no bids at all.
  • Anyone can create and price a Uniswap pool for an address that has no code yet. Revision 4.1 let the launch revert if someone had done that, which an independent reviewer showed would let anyone block the launch for ever for about $100. The token's address now does not exist and cannot be computed before the launch block, so there is nothing to prepare against. A block builder can still interfere inside that one block, which delays a launch attempt and cannot prevent the next.
  • Whoever sends the launch transaction can also buy first, at the bottom of the range. Buying a quarter of the pool's tokens there costs about 4,000 IMD, roughly $10,600. A permissionless launch cannot prevent this.
  • The liquidity position is held by the distribution contract, which has no code path that withdraws it. The trading fees it earns are collected by anyone: the IMD part joins the buyback below and the token part is burned.
  • IMD's launch factory was checked and is not used. It can issue a token with no owner and no mint, and its trading fee can go to a contract. But only IMD's own key can call it, it always gives 10% of supply to seat holders, and it would hold the position itself.
  • It is a market and a tie to IMD. It raises no money for the project: what buyers pay stays in the pool.
  • It is priced in IMD, a thin and volatile token (about $1.14M in its main pool, which is not locked, and an 18% move in two days this week). IMD has no mint function and a fixed supply of about 7 million across Ethereum and Base. On Ethereum it is a bridged token, and one owner key decides which bridge peers may credit it, so that key is trusted not to create IMD there. If IMD fails, this liquidity fails with it.

The 20%: auction and buyback

  1. The distributor releases 20% of every fee receipt into a basket evenly over seven days, so the basket's value never jumps. For fees paid in the pool's own token, the 20% is burned directly. For fees paid in IMD, it goes straight to the buyback in step 3.
  2. One continuous descending-price auction sells the whole basket for IMD. The price falls in a straight line from a starting price to a floor over one day and waits at the floor. Whoever accepts the price first pays it in IMD and takes the assets they name. The next starting price is twice the price just paid. The starting price of the very first sale and the floor are fixed in the build.
  3. The IMD is spent buying the token from the locked pool, one step every 12 hours, and the bought tokens are burned.
  4. Each step is at most half of the pool's fee rate times the locked position's virtual IMD reserve, measured from the position's own fixed liquidity and never from a balance someone could inflate. A step of the full product is the size at which a sandwich starts to pay, so a whole day's buying stays at that line.

The owner's decision was 20% into locked liquidity. This does not add liquidity in the technical sense: it moves the price up the locked position's curve and burns what it buys. The IMD ends up in the pool as bids. It stays there as long as the locked position is the liquidity in range. If someone else adds liquidity at the current price, part of each step is bought from them and that part of the IMD leaves with them, in exchange for tokens that are burned all the same.

Version 4 added the IMD as one-sided liquidity at the current price, which the buyer of the auction could set and then sell into. The buyback reaches nearly the same end without that opening. No price feed and no key is involved in any step. Anyone who sees IMD waiting for the buyback can buy ahead of it and sell later. That is ordinary speculation with price risk, and the slow pace is what keeps it from being a sure thing.

How private rewards work

  1. The pool keeps one public number, a reward index, which grows every second by that second's emission divided by the weighted total shielded. Each asset's weight is fixed in the build, because an immutable pool has no price oracle. The divisor never falls below a quarter of what the pool can hold, so a nearly empty pool cannot pay a huge rate.
  2. A note records, hidden inside its commitment, the index value when it was created. For a deposit the pool writes the exact value at activation. For notes made by a spend, the proof uses a checkpoint that anyone can refresh once an hour.
  3. When the note is spent, the proof computes what it earned: its value times its asset's weight times how far the index has moved. The amount is carried into the new notes as a hidden field.
  4. When the owner wants the tokens, any spend can turn the carried amount into a token note. Every spend has that output slot whether it is used or not, so nobody can tell a claim from a payment. Tornado's reward scheme leaked deposit age at exactly that step.

A reward follows value multiplied by time, so splitting a deposit gains nothing. Checkpoints always round against the spender, so the total paid can never exceed the emission, which the independent review checked and confirmed. The token has its own withdrawal limit inside the pool, so a mistake in the reward logic can at worst drain the reward budget released so far and any tokens users have deposited, and never another asset.

Rewards exist to gather a first crowd and to spread the token to real users. They do not keep users. The evidence is that people who come for yield leave when it ends, so the product has to earn the rest. Emissions run two years and grow in step with the caps. Otherwise most of the users' share would go to whoever filled a 25 ETH pool in its first months. Whoever fills a cap early still collects that step's rewards, and paying the 0.2% entry fee is all it costs them. At the starting valuation the reward is small: about 0.55% a year on a full pool, and four times that on a pool a quarter full or less.

IMD is listed with full reward weight: at the prices on the day the parameters are frozen, an IMD note earns the same per dollar as an ETH note. Its cap is small, 2,500 IMD rising to 80,000, so a full IMD cap earns about 4% of emissions when the pool is full and at most about 16% while the other assets are nearly empty. USDT and USDC count one dollar as 1/2,000 of an ETH. Every weight is a fixed exchange rate and will drift from the market.
