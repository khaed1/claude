Screening

A screening batch is the output of a deterministic program. Its inputs are a published criteria file, finalized chain data as of the start of the day, list files pinned by hash, and flags that screeners have posted on-chain. Any node can run it, and two honest nodes with the same data get the same batch. Each screener commits to a hash of its batch and reveals it later the same day, and the registry accepts a batch when two thirds of the active screeners have revealed the same one.

// What screeners agree on, once per one-day epoch. Computed from finalized chain data as of the epoch's start.
Batch {
  uint32  epoch;
  bytes32 criteriaHash;   // keccak256 of the criteria file below
  uint64  upTo;           // highest deposit id that was at least one day old at the epoch's start
  Word[]  words;          // refusal bitmap, 256 deposits per word, for each word that differs from the registry's
  bytes32 dataCid;        // IPFS file: every refused deposit and the rule that refused it
}

  • Who screens. Anyone who holds an IMD seat and stakes the token can register, and counts from 30 days later. There is no curator and no allowlist.
  • What it decides. Whether a deposit may be activated. It never decides whether the depositor keeps the money: a deposit can be refunded to its sender at any time before activation.
  • When. At entry. A batch covers only deposits that were already a day old when its day began, so whatever a screener learns in the first day after a deposit can still stop it. A refusal can be added or lifted until the moment of activation. After activation a note carries no label, so private transfers need no screening proof.
  • How fresh information gets in. A list cannot know about a theft from this morning. An active screener can post a flag on-chain against an address, and the published rule refuses deposits from an address that enough screeners have flagged. The quorum is a share of the active screeners, flags expire, and a flag reaches only the flagged address, so three seats cannot have an exchange's customers refused.
  • Changing the criteria. Lists go stale, so criteria files are versioned. Nodes adopt a new file when two thirds of active screeners have signalled its hash on-chain before the epoch starts. Nobody coordinates this: it is a rule in the node software, and the registry sees only batch hashes.
  • How screeners are paid. 40% of the pool's fee and 15% of the token, split equally among the screeners who revealed the accepted batch that day. Never per withdrawal, because that would reward approving more.
  • If screeners disappear. Anyone can remove a screener that has not revealed for seven days. If fewer than seven remain, or no batch reaches two thirds, new deposits wait or are refunded. Everything already in the pool keeps working.
  • If a minority blocks. A group that reveals something other than the majority's batch for seven days running can be removed by anyone, so a third of the screeners can delay deposits for about a week and no longer.
  • If screeners are bought. Whoever holds two thirds can approve a deposit that is at least a day old, and can refuse everyone else's deposits. Whoever holds a majority for a week can remove the rest and then do the same. They could not move funds, and anyone can recompute the batch and see what they did. Refusing everyone else matters because rewards go to whoever is inside: control of the registry is also control of who earns the users' 45%.

What screening at entry does not do. It stops funds that a list or a flag identifies within a day or two of the deposit. Stolen funds that nobody has identified by then enter and cannot be removed afterwards. Railgun's check has the same limit with a one-hour wait. Privacy Pools can still exclude a deposit until it is withdrawn, and is stronger on this point. The criteria are public, so a careful thief can test a deposit against them in advance.

What the registry does not prove. It is as independent as the seats are: one holder with many seats is many screeners, and one address holds 77 of the 2,000. Commit and reveal stops a screener copying another's answer from the chain. It cannot stop screeners sharing an answer off-chain, so agreement may reflect fewer computations than signatures. And tracing flows needs an archive-grade data source, which in practice means a handful of providers shape what every screener sees.
