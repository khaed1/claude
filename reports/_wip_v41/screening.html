Screening

A screening batch is the output of a deterministic program. Its inputs are a published criteria file, public chain data at a named block, list files pinned by hash, and flags that screeners have posted on-chain. Any node can run it, and any two honest nodes get the same batch. Each screener commits to a hash of its batch and reveals it later the same day, and the registry accepts a batch when two thirds of the active screeners have revealed the same one.

// What screeners agree on, once per 7,200-block epoch. Computed from chain data at the epoch's first block.
Batch {
  uint32  epoch;
  bytes32 criteriaHash;   // keccak256 of the criteria file below
  uint64  upTo;           // highest deposit id made before the epoch's first block
  Word[]  words;          // refusal bitmap, 256 deposits per word, for each word that differs from what is on-chain
  bytes32 dataCid;        // IPFS file: every refused deposit and the rule that refused it
}

  • Who screens. Anyone who holds an IMD seat and stakes the token can register, and counts from 30 days later. There is no curator and no allowlist.
  • What it decides. Whether a deposit may be activated. It never decides whether the depositor keeps the money: a deposit can be refunded to its sender at any time before activation.
  • When. At entry, with one to two days for screeners to refuse stolen funds. A refusal can be added or lifted until the moment of activation. After activation a note carries no label, so private transfers need no screening proof.
  • How fresh information gets in. A list cannot know about a theft from this morning. A screener can post a flag on-chain against an address, and the published rule refuses deposits that enough screeners have flagged. The judgement is the screener's. The rule that turns flags into refusals is fixed and public, so the batch stays recomputable.
  • Changing the criteria. Lists go stale, so criteria files are versioned. Nodes adopt a new file when two thirds of active screeners have signalled its hash on-chain before the epoch starts. Nobody coordinates this: it is a rule in the node software, and the registry sees only batch hashes.
  • How screeners are paid. 40% of the pool's fee and 15% of the token, split equally among the screeners who revealed the accepted batch that day. Never per withdrawal, because that would reward approving more. Commit and reveal means a screener cannot copy another's answer.
  • If screeners disappear. A screener that stops revealing is dropped from the count after seven days. If fewer than seven remain, or no batch reaches two thirds, new deposits wait or are refunded. Everything already in the pool keeps working.
  • If screeners are bought. Two thirds of them could approve a bad deposit that is at least a day old. They could not move funds, and anyone can recompute the batch and see what they did. One third could delay new deposits for as long as they keep it up.

What screening at entry does not do. It stops funds that a list or a flag identifies before the deposit is activated. Stolen funds that nobody has identified within one to two days enter and cannot be removed afterwards. Railgun's check has the same limit with a one-hour wait. Privacy Pools can still exclude a deposit until it is withdrawn, and is stronger on this point. The criteria are public, so a careful thief can test a deposit against them in advance. And the registry is as independent as the seats are: one holder with many seats is many screeners.

Why screen at all

A pool that accepts every deposit is possible. Tornado Cash is one, and what happened to it is the reason this pool does not copy it.

  • Thieves arrive first. A Cambridge study found attackers used Tornado Cash in 78% of Ethereum incidents through mid-2025. The wallet behind the Binance hack of October 2026 was funded through it.
  • Honest users pay for it. Exchanges flag or freeze funds that come out of an unscreened pool, and some block accounts with only indirect exposure. A private withdrawal nobody will accept is worth little.
  • Issuers can freeze the pool. Circle froze the USDC inside Tornado Cash's pool in 2022. A pool known to hold stolen funds invites the same.
  • The fee depends on it. This pool takes a fee and has a token. Profiting from every deposit, stolen ones included, is the pattern the Tornado Cash and Samourai prosecutions turned on. Railgun and 0xbow charge fees, screen, and have not been charged.
  • Wallets are less likely to offer it otherwise. People reach a pool through a wallet app, and the launch gate requires one with real users. Wallet teams carry their own legal and app-store exposure, and the wallets that offer privacy today, Railway and Ambire, chose a screened pool. This is not absolute: the Ethereum Foundation's Kohaku kit has a Tornado Cash plugin in alpha.
  • There would be no reason to exist. An unscreened immutable pool already exists and holds about $780M. A new one would be the same thing with no crowd.

Screening does not weaken an honest user's privacy. It looks only at the deposit, which is public in any pool, and after activation nothing connects the deposit to what happens inside. What it costs is a wait of one to two days.

Before the pool exists, the same screening program serves as a second opinion on Privacy Pools v1 withdrawals. The user makes a side proof with Privacy Pools' own circuit, and anyone checks it against the verifier contract already deployed there. That is how screeners build a public record first.
