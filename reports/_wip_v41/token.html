Token and fee

The first two versions of this spec forbade a protocol fee and a token. On 10 November 2026 the owner decided to add both, to keep the pool fully immutable, and to keep the project decentralized. This section gives them the only shape that fits those three decisions, and says plainly what they change.

The fee

  • 0.2% on the way in and 0.2% on the way out, taken in the asset itself. Together that is 0.4%, against Railgun's 0.5%. Transfers inside the pool are free.
  • The entry fee is taken when a deposit is activated, so a refunded deposit is returned in full.
  • The rate and the destination are fixed in code. Nobody can raise, lower or redirect it.
  • It goes to an immutable distributor: 40% to token stakers in proportion to stake, 40% split equally among the screeners who revealed that day's accepted batch, and 20% into the token's own liquidity for good.
  • Stakers are paid in the asset the fee was taken in, evenly over the seven days after each sweep, so staking for one block around a sweep earns nothing extra.

The public pool

  • At launch the distribution contract places 25% of supply as one-sided liquidity in a Uniswap v3 pool against IMD, at the 1% fee tier. Buyers bring IMD and the price rises as they buy. There is no ETH pair.
  • The range starts at a valuation of 48,000 IMD for the whole supply, about $127,000 with IMD at $2.64, and runs upward without a practical limit so the pool never runs out of tokens to buy. With a share f of the pool's tokens bought, the pool holds 12,000 × f / (1 − f) IMD and the price is the start divided by (1 − f)². At half bought that is 12,000 IMD and four times the start. The simulation tabulates it.
  • A one-sided pool starts with no IMD in it, so sellers can only sell into what buyers have paid, and below the starting price there are no bids at all.
  • The token's address is known before it exists, and anyone can create a Uniswap pool for it and set a price first. The launch code handles a pool that already exists and brings a wrong price back to the start before placing the position.
  • The liquidity position is held by the distribution contract, which has no code path that withdraws it. The trading fees it earns are collected by anyone: the IMD part joins the buyback below and the token part is burned.
  • It is a market and a tie to IMD. It raises no money for the project: what buyers pay stays in the pool.
  • It is priced in IMD, a thin and volatile token (about $1.27M in its main pool, and an 18% move in two days this week). If IMD fails, this liquidity fails with it.

The 20%: auction and buyback

  1. The distributor keeps 20% of every fee receipt in a basket of the pool's assets. For fees paid in the pool's own token, the 20% is burned directly.
  2. One continuous descending-price auction sells the whole basket for IMD. The price falls in a straight line from a starting price to a floor over one day and waits at the floor. Whoever accepts the price first pays it in IMD and takes the assets they name. The next starting price is twice the price just paid. The starting price of the very first sale and the floor are fixed at deployment.
  3. The IMD is spent buying the token from the locked pool in small steps, at most one every 25 blocks, and the bought tokens are burned. Because the position can never be withdrawn and the bought tokens no longer exist, that IMD stays in the pool as bids for good.
  4. Each step is at most half of the pool's fee rate times its IMD reserves. Below the full product a sandwich costs more in fees than it gains, so the limit leaves a margin of two.

Version 4 added the IMD as one-sided liquidity at the current price, which the buyer of the auction could set and then sell into. The buyback reaches the same end without that opening. No price feed and no key is involved in any step.

How private rewards work

  1. The pool keeps one public number, a reward index, which grows every block by that block's emission divided by the weighted total shielded. Each asset's weight is fixed at deployment, because an immutable pool has no price oracle. The divisor never falls below a quarter of what the pool can hold, so a nearly empty pool cannot pay a huge rate.
  2. A note records, hidden inside its commitment, the index value when it was created. For a deposit the pool writes the exact value at activation. For notes made by a spend, the proof uses a checkpoint taken every 300 blocks.
  3. When the note is spent, the proof computes what it earned: its value times its asset's weight times how far the index has moved. The amount is carried into the new notes as a hidden field.
  4. When the owner wants the tokens, any spend can turn the carried amount into a token note. Every spend has that output slot whether it is used or not, so nobody can tell a claim from a payment. Tornado's reward scheme leaked deposit age at exactly that step.

A reward follows value multiplied by time, so splitting a deposit gains nothing. Checkpoints always round against the spender, so the total paid can never exceed the emission. The token has its own withdrawal limit inside the pool, so a mistake in the reward logic can at worst drain the reward budget released so far and never another asset.

Rewards exist to gather a first crowd and to spread the token to real users. They do not keep users. The evidence is that people who come for yield leave when it ends, so the product has to earn the rest. Emissions run two years and grow in step with the caps. Otherwise most of the users' share would go to whoever filled a 25 ETH pool in its first months. At the starting valuation the reward is small: about 0.6% a year on a full pool, and four times that on a pool a quarter full or less.

What this changes

Legal position. A fee and a token make the project look like a business to financial-crime and securities enforcement. In the Tornado Cash and Samourai prosecutions, the money the developers made was part of the case. Railgun and 0xbow charge fees and have not been charged; both pair the fee with real screening. This design pairs it with screening enforced by the contract and sends no fee to a team address. It does offer 25% of the token to the public through a pool, and offering tokens to the public is the step regulators target most directly. Stakers receive a share of fees, which is the feature securities law looks at first. Those choices reduce the exposure in some places and add to it in others. They do not remove it.

Founder allocation and pseudonymity. The founder's 5% sits at a public address for two years. The review recommended choosing one of two options, pseudonymity with no special allocation or an allocation held openly. The owner chose a pseudonymous allocation. It is recorded here as a decision, not as a recommendation, and it should be put to a lawyer before any deployment.

This is not legal advice. A lawyer reviews the fee, the token, the public pool and the founder allocation before launch, and before the canary, which places a token pool of its own.

Cost. The token contracts go through the same IMD review, proofs and canary as the pool. A token is worth little until the pool has volume: Railgun's 0.25% brings in about $312,000 a month at $106M, and the caps hold this pool's fee income under about $400 a month for its first 90 days (see the simulation).
