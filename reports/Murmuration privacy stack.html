Murmuration: a swarm-operated privacy stack

Ethereum already has the cryptography for private transfers. What it lacks is independence at the edges. Railgun's main wallet trusts four broadcasters, Privacy Pools has one screening operator, and both pools keep an upgrade key. The holders of IMD's 1,999 seats can widen those edges with software that already exists. Revision 3 keeps that lean periphery and puts a pool of its own at the centre: fully immutable, screened, with fixed-size exits, a fee of 0.2% in and 0.2% out and a token that pays the people who run it. Revision 4 makes the pool a public test of the swarm: IMD agents build and audit it with no external audit firm, behind very low caps.

Thesis: services first, then a pool of its own

Three findings from the first version stand. Anonymity sets are the scarcest resource on Ethereum: Privacy Pools v2 holds 583 deposits on mainnet three weeks after launch. Every compromise of Tornado Cash came through its mutable periphery (governance, frontend, relayer registry, fee token), the US prosecution leaned on the same parts, and the immutable pools were the only part a court protected. Every live privacy product still concentrates some role in one operator.

The adversarial re-review of 9 and 10 November 2026 added a fourth finding, and it changes what should be built. Operator capacity is not what Ethereum privacy is short of. Over the three days to 10 November, Railgun on mainnet carried about 286 spends a day, one every five minutes. Seven sending addresses carried 80% of them, and every broadcaster on the network together earned a margin of roughly $480 a month at current gas prices. What is short is independence and distribution. Since version 5.26.1 (5 October 2026) Railway Wallet only selects broadcasters on a four-address allowlist in its remote configuration. Privacy Pools' contracts accept only the latest root from 0xbow's own postman. A thousand idle machines change nothing until a wallet is willing to choose them.

So Murmuration ships three things. An admission rule that a wallet can verify by itself: seat ownership plus a delegation signed by the seat holder. A selection policy that does not hand all traffic to the cheapest quote. And public data (pool snapshots, screening sets, pool-health figures) built so that any two independent nodes produce the same bytes. IMD supplies the operators and a cost per identity. No running part of the protocol goes through IMD's control plane, no user funds touch IMD's contracts, and no operator is paid in IMD's token. IMD's agents are used in the ordinary way, as paid builders and reviewers of open-source code.

Design rule 1. Nothing a seat produces is load-bearing for funds or for privacy. Every output is either checked against the chain by the wallet (relays, snapshots) or advisory (screening attestations, health data). A seat costs about 0.70 ETH and can be resold, so a seat signature is a quality filter and never a security root. This rule is what lets the periphery work without bonds, slashing or registries. In the pool, screeners stake and their roots are accepted on-chain, and even there a captured panel can approve a bad deposit and cannot move funds.

Design rule 2. Everything in the periphery must work with Railgun and Privacy Pools as they are deployed today. That is how the project earns wallet distribution before its own pool exists.

Revision 3 records the owner's decision of 10 November 2026 to make a pool of the project's own the main product again. The reason is positioning. Tornado Cash is immutable and unscreened, so its exits are treated as tainted. Railgun and Privacy Pools are screened and keep admin keys. No pool is both immutable and screened. The Murmuration pool is designed to be that, and to add two things neither has: fixed-size exits on top of a private ledger, and note encryption that a future quantum computer cannot open. It launches with no users, and that is its largest weakness. The comparison states both sides.

Revision 4 records two more decisions by the owner on 10 November 2026. First, the project relies on IMD's own audits and pays no external audit firm, as a test of whether the swarm can build and secure a system like this. Second, 15% of the token goes into a public one-sided pool paired with IMD at launch, and every token schedule runs two years. The review's position is unchanged: an AI-only audit of an immutable pool is the largest technical risk on this page. The design answers it with proofs a machine checks, a canary anyone may break, and caps low enough that a failure is small.

The six services

The section keeps the first version's numbering so the two can be compared. Two services are kept in simpler form, two are replaced by client-side or public-data mechanisms, and two are cut. Each card states the guarantee that is lost by the change. The evidence is in Review findings.

Service 1

Broadcaster mesh

KeptProtocol reused unchangedNo contract

A seat holder runs Railgun's own broadcaster (version 8.2.0, ISC licence) on Railgun's own Waku cluster, and the open-source Privacy Pools relayer beside it. Murmuration adds no wire protocol. It adds a way for a wallet to tell that a broadcaster is backed by a seat, and a selection policy for wallets built on Railgun's client library: accept fee signers that hold a seat, keep those inside a fee band, pick one at random.

The mesh does not change what a broadcaster sees. A Railgun broadcaster decrypts the whole transaction before anyone else, including an unshield's recipient and a swap's calldata. It can delay or refuse. It cannot redirect funds, because its fee is a note proven inside the same transaction. What more independent broadcasters buy is a smaller share of traffic for any one of them and fewer ways for all of them to refuse at once. Hiding the sender's IP is the wallet transport's job.

Service 2

Panel ASP

Kept as an overlayNo contractNo slashing

A screening set is the output of a deterministic program: published criteria, public chain data at a named block, and list files pinned by hash. Any node can run it, and any two honest nodes get the same root. A panel attestation is a bundle of signatures by distinct seat holders over that root. The relying party decides how many signatures it wants and can always recompute the root itself, so the signatures are a convenience and the reproducibility is the guarantee.

No pool accepts a second ASP today. Privacy Pools v1 and v2 both check the latest root from 0xbow's postman and carry no ASP identifier. What does work, with no new contract or circuit, is a side proof on Privacy Pools v1: the user proves membership of their deposit's label in a panel's set with the existing withdrawal circuit, and anyone verifies it with a call to the verifier contract that is already deployed. The overlay cannot unlock a withdrawal 0xbow refuses. It gives a recipient a second, published-criteria opinion about a withdrawal 0xbow allowed.

Service 4

Private read layer

ReplacedPublic snapshots, no query to hide

A shielded wallet does not need to ask anyone a private question if it can download the whole public log. Each node builds a deterministic daily snapshot of every supported pool's events (commitments, ciphertexts, nullifiers, Privacy Pools labels) and pins it to IPFS. Independent nodes produce the same content identifier, which is itself the cross-check. The wallet fetches the same files as every other user, rebuilds the Merkle root and compares it with the root on-chain. A wrong snapshot fails that check. A withheld one is fetched from another mirror.

For the few reads that remain interactive, the wallet uses the Ethereum Foundation's anon-rpc standard (Tor, Nym or Nox workers) with a Helios light client. Neither is a seat service.

Service 5

Anonymity-set stewardship agents

ReducedA dataset, with advice computed on the device

Nodes publish a daily pool-health file per pool, built by the same deterministic rule as snapshots: activity by asset and amount band, exact-amount collisions, the share of exits a published first-in-first-out heuristic would match, concentration among depositors and among broadcasters. The wallet downloads the file and works out its own warnings and a suggested delay. No request describing a planned transaction ever leaves the device.

The first version's advisory API would have received the amount, a hash of the recipient and the time before the transaction existed. A hash of an address can be reversed by trying every address that exists. The scheduling service would have known the true creation time of every delayed exit. Decoy flows had no source of funds and looked like structuring. All three are removed.

Service 6

Threshold selective disclosure

CutReplaced by a per-transaction receipt the user makes

A panel holding shares of a decryption key is a standing decryption authority. The first version called it revocable, but revocation meant trusting enough panelists to delete their shares. Any quorum of seats could read every opted-in user's records, and the spec's own legal gate said there would be no network viewing key.

The user already holds everything needed to disclose one transaction. A disclosure receipt opens the notes of a single transaction to a named recipient: the commitment preimages, their Merkle paths, and for Privacy Pools the link from deposit label to withdrawal. The recipient checks it against the chain. No operator is involved and nothing persists that would need revoking.

This changes one of the project's standing rules, deliberately. The rule was "disclosure is user-held, opt-in and revocable". It is now "user-held, opt-in and scoped to one transaction, with no standing grant". That is stronger than revocable.

Disclosure without the user's device, which is the property a backdoor has. It is not offered

Layer 0 · the main product, phase 2

The immutable pool

Core product in revision 3Fully immutableNew circuits

A shielded ledger with one tree for all supported assets. Balances are private notes, transfers inside the pool are private and free, and nothing has an owner, a pause or an upgrade path. Four features define it.

  • Immutable and screened. A deposit can be spent privately only after a published-criteria screening set approves it, and never sooner than about a day. A refused depositor can always take the deposit back publicly. Screening can stop a thief from using the pool. It cannot touch anyone's funds.
  • Fixed-size exits. Inside the pool amounts are arbitrary. Leaving it, funds come out only in standard sizes, as in Tornado Cash. Matching a deposit to a withdrawal by amount stops working.
  • Quantum-resistant note encryption. Encrypted notes sit on-chain forever. They are encrypted with a hybrid of X25519 and ML-KEM, so recording them now and decrypting them later does not work.
  • Private rewards. Notes earn the project token for time spent shielded, collected inside the proof when a note is spent. There is no claim transaction to observe.

Revision 2 limited the pool to a fork of Privacy Pools v1 with no new circuits. Revision 3 gives that up: internal transfers, fixed exits and rewards need circuits that do not exist yet. That brings back the largest risk in the document, a soundness bug that nobody can patch. In revision 4 that risk is carried without an external audit. The containment measures are in the pool design.

Specs and technical implementation

Formats below are proposed, not deployed. The periphery is off-chain data formats and client-side rules. The pool, the token and the fee distributor are the only contracts. Everything is written so the project team can publish code and run none of it.

Identity without a registry

Admission is derived, never granted, and it is checked by whoever relies on it. A seat is the identity.md NFT at 0x0000ec93…ec1d. Its source is verified, it is not a proxy, and its owner() is the CREATE2 deployment proxy it was created through, so its owner-only functions have no key behind them. The holder signs one delegation from the wallet that holds the seat. The node then works with a separate operational key, so the wallet holding the NFT never sits on a server.

// EIP-712 typed data. Domain { name: "Murmuration", version: "2", chainId: 1 }; no verifyingContract.
SeatDelegation {
  uint256 seatId;          // identity.md tokenId
  address opKey;           // secp256k1 key the node signs with. Not the holder key, not IMD's device key
  string  railgunAddress;  // 0zk address that signs fee messages and receives fee notes ("" if unused)
  address ppFeeRecipient;  // Privacy Pools relay fee recipient (0x0 if unused)
  uint8   duties;          // bit 0 relay, bit 1 mirror, bit 2 attest
  uint64  issuedAt;        // the highest issuedAt per seat wins
  uint64  notAfter;        // at most issuedAt + 90 days
}

Verification, by any wallet or relying party

  1. Read ownerOf(seatId) at a recent finalized block, through a light client or two independent RPC endpoints.
  2. Check the signature against that owner. Use ecrecover when the owner has no code and an ERC-1271 isValidSignature call when it has. The second path covers Safes and EIP-7702 accounts, and stays correct if EIP-8151 changes ecrecover for accounts with code.
  3. Check now ≤ notAfter ≤ issuedAt + 90 days. A transferred seat voids its delegation automatically, because the owner no longer matches.
  4. Group seats by holder and count each holder once. Optionally require that the holder has held the seat for 30 days, read from Transfer logs.

Publication

Delegations need a public bulletin board, not a trusted registry. The default is an attestation on the existing Ethereum Attestation Service contract (0xA1207F3B…e587, about 231,000 gas each) that carries the signed struct. Clients verify the inner signature themselves, so EAS is relied on for availability and nothing else. The same struct can travel over any other channel.

What a seat is worth as an identity

  • Cost per identity: the floor, 0.70 ETH on 10 November 2026 (about $1,490), down from 0.796 on 3 November. A seat can be resold, so the real cost of an attack is capital tied up for its duration plus price impact. Seven-day volume on 8 November was about $79,000, fewer than 50 seats.
  • Distinct holders are a convenience, not a defence. A holder can move seats to fresh wallets for the price of a transfer. The number that matters is seats multiplied by the floor. The 30-day holding filter raises the time cost and nothing more.
  • Not in the trust path: IMD's ERC-8004 adapter (a proxy upgradeable by a three-of-four Safe), the ERC-8004 registries (proxies owned by one externally owned account), the Ed25519 device key in IMD's config file, and IMD's API. The first version read or reused all four.

Broadcaster mesh protocol

What a node runs, unchanged

  • Railgun broadcaster 8.2.0 from gitlab.com/railgun-protocol. The GitHub copy stops at 8.0.0 and points at the wrong Waku cluster.
  • nwaku 0.37.1 on Railgun's cluster 5, shard 1, with Railgun's content topics (/railgun/v2/0-1-fees/json and the matching transact topics). That cluster does not use RLN.
  • A PPOI node URL and an RPC endpoint of the operator's own. The broadcaster refuses any transaction without a valid proof of innocence for the required list.
  • The open-source Privacy Pools relayer, if the operator also relays there.

What Murmuration adds, in the wallet

Railgun's client library already takes a list of trusted fee signers and drops quotes outside a band around their average. Railway fills that list from its own remote configuration. The policy below fills it from seat delegations and changes two rules.

signers  = feeMessages.filter(m => validDelegation(m.railgunAddress))   // steps 1 to 4 above
byHolder = oneSignerPerHolder(signers)                                 // a holder counts once
band     = byHolder.filter(s => 0.90*median <= s.fee <= 1.30*median)   // Railgun's band, around the median
pick     = uniformRandom(band)                                         // fresh local randomness per attempt
prove(tx, feeNoteTo = pick.railgunAddress); send(pick)
on refusal or timeout:
    remember(pick, 24h)                    // local memory only; nothing is reported anywhere
    pick again and prove again             // the fee note names one broadcaster, so a retry is a new proof
  • Median, not mean. A few hostile seats cannot drag a median the way they drag an average.
  • Random, not cheapest. Railgun's default takes the lowest fee. With open admission that hands every transaction to whoever quotes one percent under the rest. One seat, 0.70 ETH, would see all traffic first.
  • No seed. The first version derived the draw from a wallet secret and the nullifier. A retry would then land on the same broadcaster that just refused.

What a broadcaster can and cannot do

  • It cannot redirect funds or take a larger fee. Its fee is a commitment proven inside the transaction. Railgun's bound parameters do not name a submitter, so a copier who lands the transaction first only wastes the broadcaster's gas.
  • It sees the full transaction before inclusion: the unshield recipient, the calldata of a RelayAdapt swap, the PPOI proofs. It can refuse, delay, or trade ahead of a swap. Wallets should set tight minimum-output limits on relayed swaps.
  • An adversary with k seats among N honest broadcasters sees about k/(N+k) of relayed traffic, for k times 0.70 ETH of resalable capital. With ten seats among thirty honest ones that is a quarter of traffic for about 7 ETH.
  • It does not learn the sender's IP if the wallet reaches Waku through Tor. Railway Wallet ships no Tor or mixnet today, and the mesh cannot fix that from the operator side.

Panel ASP: attestations, criteria and the overlay proof

Nothing here is a contract. The first version's PanelASP.sol is removed. In the Murmuration pool the same attestation format is what the screening registry accepts on-chain.

// Output of the deterministic screener, signed by each attesting node's opKey (EIP-712)
SetAttestation {
  bytes32 criteriaHash;   // keccak256 of the criteria file below
  uint256 scope;          // the pool's scope value (Privacy Pools) or a pool identifier
  uint64  atBlock;        // finalized block the chain data was read at
  uint256 root;           // LeanIMT Poseidon root over approved labels, same format the pool's circuit expects
  bytes32 dataCid;        // IPFS file: approved labels, excluded labels, and the rule that excluded each
}

Criteria file

{ "name": "sanctions-plus-two-hops", "version": 1,
  "lists": [ { "name": "chainalysis-sanctions-oracle", "source": "eth_call:0x40C57923924B5c5c5455c48D93317139ADDaC8fb@atBlock" },
             { "name": "ofac-sdn", "source": "ipfs://…", "sha256": "…" } ],
  "rules": [ { "deny": "listed(depositor)" },
             { "deny": "hopsFromListed(depositor) <= 2 && age(deposit) < 7d" },
             { "allow": "default" } ] }
  • Every input is reproducible. A list is either on-chain state at atBlock or a file pinned by hash. A source that cannot be replayed later is not allowed in a criteria file.
  • The relying party sets the threshold. A wallet might want five distinct holders, an exchange might want named signers it knows. There is no protocol-wide t-of-n and no on-chain acceptance.
  • Disagreement is visible. Two different roots for the same criteria hash and block mean someone is wrong, and anyone can find out who by running the screener.
  • Nobody is paid per use. The first version paid panelists a share of each withdrawal that used their root. Users choose the panel, so that pays the most lenient panel the most.

Overlay proof on Privacy Pools v1

  1. The screener rebuilds the label set from Deposited events. In v1 a label is keccak256(scope, nonce) mod p and is emitted in the clear. A check on 9 and 10 November recomputed 6,498 labels of the ETH pool and found 5,581 of them in 0xbow's current list, whose top node equals the root on-chain.
  2. The user generates a second Groth16 proof with the unmodified v1 withdrawal circuit, setting the ASP root input to the panel's root.
  3. Anyone verifies it with an eth_call to the v1 verifier already deployed at 0x022891F9…7D6d. A replay of a real mainnet proof returned true even though its root was no longer the latest, and false when the root was altered.
  4. The side proof exposes the same nullifier hash as the real withdrawal. That ties the attestation to one withdrawal and reveals nothing about which deposit it came from.

Limits. It cannot unlock anything 0xbow's ASP refuses, because the pool checks ENTRYPOINT.latestRoot() and nothing else. It does not work for v2 without the depositor's help, since v2 labels are private. Railgun screens with PPOI lists rather than ASP roots, and its software hard-codes one required list, so the overlay does not apply there. No recipient has asked for such a proof yet. It stays in scope because it costs no contract and is the only place multi-party, published-criteria screening can build a record before any pool depends on it.

If a contract ever has to check a panel

A Safe 1.4.1 whose owners are the panelists already acts as a t-of-n ERC-1271 signer: isValidSignature(hash, signatures) verified seven signatures for 64,279 gas in simulation, with no new code. Owners sign Safe's message wrapper, signatures are sorted by owner address, and validity follows the current owner set.

Why slashing is gone

  • The second slashing condition could not be implemented. It needed a Merkle proof against "the list's own published root". Neither the Chainalysis oracle nor the SDN file publishes one, and proving past oracle state on-chain needs new proof-verification code.
  • The stake was small: seven bonds of 0.5 ETH, about $7,500, against deposits of any size.
  • Restaking is not off the shelf. EigenLayer and Symbiotic both leave the slashing decision to a contract the project would have to write and audit.

  • Verified by the wallet. The wallet rebuilds the commitment tree from the chunks and compares the root with the pool's on-chain root at toBlock.
  • Nothing to hide. Every user fetches the same chunks. A mirror or gateway learns that someone uses the pool, and no more. Fetch over Tor to hide that too.
  • Covers history expiry. Pools' logs stay available even when full nodes stop serving old ones.
  • Integration. Railgun's engine takes its quick-sync source as a parameter. The exact hook in engine 9.9.1 is still to be confirmed.

Remaining interactive reads (gas price, a public balance before shielding) go through anon-rpc and Helios in the wallet. Helios verifies answers and does not hide the question, which is why transport privacy comes from the anon-rpc worker.

Assurance without an external audit

The owner's decision is to rely on IMD and see whether the swarm can secure what it builds. Wallets, L2BEAT and exchanges look for a named firm's report, so adoption will be slower without one. Four measures make the test fair and keep a failure small.

  • IMD audit panels on everything. Every contract and circuit goes through IMD's audit template, with each finding and its fix public. Reviewers built on the same models as the builders tend to share blind spots, which is why the next three measures do not depend on reviewers.
  • Machine-checked proofs. The swarm writes formal proofs that the transfer circuit cannot create value or spend a note twice, and anyone can re-run the proof checker. A checked proof holds whoever wrote it. Open-source analysers for under-constrained circuits are run in public as well.
  • A canary. The same bytecode is deployed on mainnet 90 days before launch and holds at least 10 ETH, deposited as an ordinary user would. Anyone who can break the circuits can take it. No judge is needed, and funds that survive are withdrawn normally.
  • Very low caps. The pool opens at about 25 ETH per asset and doubles every 90 days, reaching about 6,400 ETH after two years. The most that can be lost at any time is the current cap.

Nothing in the research shows that IMD has audited a zero-knowledge circuit before. This launch is that test.

Planning figure for gas: a transfer should cost about what a Railgun spend does, 1.17M gas or roughly $0.50 at 0.2 gwei, plus the larger ciphertexts. This is a reference, not a measurement.

Legal gate: requirements every component must pass

The record, stated carefully. The Fifth Circuit's Van Loon ruling protects only immutable contracts and is a sanctions ruling, not a criminal one. Roman Storm was convicted on one count, conspiracy to operate an unlicensed money transmitting business, under a theory that turns on knowledge. The verdict was general, the Rule 29 motion is still pending and a retrial on the hung counts is set for 26 April 2027. Prosecutors' account of his conduct included paying to host the website, maintaining the relayer network and profiting from it. The Dutch court in Pertsev's case held that Tornado Cash itself did the concealing. The DOJ's 2025 statements are discretionary. The statutory safe harbour failed cloture 49 to 50 on 15 September 2026 and lapses with this Congress on 3 January 2027.

  • The pool, the token and the fee distributor are immutable: no admin, pause, upgrade, fee switch or mint function.
  • The project team writes software and operates nothing: no node, relayer, mirror, screener or hosted frontend. The software ships as a node package and a wallet module.
  • The team does not recruit, pay or coordinate operators. Roadmap gates are observations read from the chain.
  • Changed in revision 3. A fixed fee of 0.2% in and 0.2% out and a token exist. No fee goes to a team address. The founder holds 5% of the token, vested in code over two years, at one public address.
  • Changed in revision 4. 25% of the token is offered to the public through a locked pool paired with IMD. No external audit firm reviews the code. Assurance comes from IMD audit panels, machine-checked proofs, a canary and very low caps.
  • Screening is real, published and executed by independent screeners. The pool contract enforces it at a deposit's first spend, and seat broadcasters enforce PPOI on Railgun.
  • No running part is brokered through IMD's control plane. IMD jobs are bought only to build and review open-source code. Admission is derived from on-chain seat ownership.
  • Disclosure is user-held, opt-in and scoped to one transaction. No key share exists anywhere outside the user's wallet.
  • No support channel through which the team learns about specific flows.

Two standing rules are reversed by the owner's decision of 10 November 2026: "no developer fee in any token" and "no project token". The review advised against both, and against combining a founder allocation with pseudonymity. Immutability decides whether anyone controls the code. It does not decide who profits from it, and profit is what enforcement has looked at. The reasoning and the mitigations are in Token and fee. Revision 4 adds two more departures from the review: a public token pool, and no external audit.

Operators carry the exposure the developers avoid. No guidance or ruling found says whether a fee-earning relayer is a money transmitter. FinCEN's 2019 guidance separates anonymizing software providers, who are not, from anonymizing services providers, who are, and never mentions relayers. At the Rule 29 hearing on 9 April 2026 the government called relayers "part of the cohort operating Tornado Cash... for a profit". Each operator needs advice under their own law. The first version listed "no logs" and "jurisdictional diversity" as mitigations. Both are removed, because they read as planning to avoid enforcement.

Two rules were tested against new evidence and kept. Immutability has a cost, shown when the ownerless Aztec Connect contracts were drained with no way to stop it, and the plan answers that with three small circuits, formal verification, per-asset limits, immutable caps and a fixed deposit window. Against that cost, on 7 October 2026 the EU's AML authority told the Commission that "effective control" over a DeFi arrangement may arise from "the ability to pause or modify smart contracts". The rule stays.

Token and fee

The first two versions of this spec forbade a protocol fee and a token. On 10 November 2026 the owner decided to add both, to keep the pool fully immutable, and to keep the project decentralized. This section gives them the only shape that fits those three decisions, and says plainly what they change.

The fee

  • 0.2% on the way in and 0.2% on the way out, taken in the asset itself. Together that is 0.4%, against Railgun's 0.5%. Transfers inside the pool are free.
  • The entry fee is taken when a deposit is approved and first spent, so a refused deposit is returned in full.
  • The rate and the destination are fixed in code. Nobody can raise, lower or redirect it.
  • It goes to an immutable distributor: 40% to token stakers in proportion to stake, 40% split equally among the screeners whose signatures are on that day's accepted root, and 20% into the token's own liquidity for good.
  • Screeners are paid per day of agreement with the accepted root, never per withdrawal. Paying per use would reward approving more.

The public pool

  • At launch the distribution contract places 25% of supply as one-sided liquidity in a Uniswap pool against IMD. Buyers bring IMD and the price rises as they buy. There is no ETH pair.
  • The range starts at a valuation of 48,000 IMD for the whole supply, about $127,000 with IMD at $2.64, and extends far above it so the pool never runs out of tokens to buy. Once half of the 25% has been bought, the pool holds about 12,000 IMD and the price is four times the start. These figures are arithmetic for a standard range position. They have not been tested, and the dollar values move with IMD.
  • A one-sided pool starts with no IMD in it, so sellers can only sell into what buyers have paid. To deepen it, 20% of every fee the privacy pool collects goes into this market for good. Once a day the distributor sells that share for IMD in a descending-price auction open to anyone, then adds the IMD as buy-side liquidity that can never be withdrawn. No price feed and no key is involved.
  • That mechanism is more logic in contracts that can never be patched. It is kept to those two steps, and it is covered by the same proofs and canary as the rest.
  • The liquidity position is held by the contract and can never be withdrawn. Trading fees it earns go to the fee distributor, not to any wallet.
  • It is a market and a tie to IMD. It raises no money for the project: what buyers pay stays in the pool.
  • It is priced in IMD, a thin and volatile token (about $1.27M in its main pool, and an 18% move in two days this week). If IMD fails, this liquidity fails with it.
  • IMD's launch factory could do the same in one request and pays 1% of every trade to the launching wallet. It is used only if it can issue a token with no owner and no mint function and send that fee to the distributor. Neither has been checked.

How private rewards work

  1. For each asset the pool keeps one public number, a reward index, which grows every day by that day's emission divided by the total amount shielded.
  2. A note records, hidden inside its commitment, the index value on the day it was created. The proof that creates the note shows this value matches the public one.
  3. When the note is spent, the same proof shows what it earned: its value multiplied by how far the index has moved. The pool adds the reward as a new shielded note for the same owner.
  4. Nobody sees the amount, the age or the owner, and there is no separate claim to observe. Tornado's reward scheme leaked deposit age at exactly that step.

A reward follows value multiplied by time, so splitting a deposit gains nothing. Each asset's share of emissions is fixed at deployment, because an immutable pool has no price oracle. The token has its own withdrawal limit inside the pool, so a mistake in the reward logic can at worst drain the reward budget and never another asset.

Rewards exist to gather a first crowd and to spread the token to real users. They do not keep users. The evidence is that people who come for yield leave when it ends, so the product has to earn the rest. Emissions run two years and grow in step with the caps. Otherwise most of the users' share would go to whoever filled a 25 ETH pool in its first months.

What this changes

Legal position. A fee and a token make the project look like a business to financial-crime and securities enforcement. In the Tornado Cash and Samourai prosecutions, the money the developers made was part of the case. Railgun and 0xbow charge fees and have not been charged; both pair the fee with real screening. This design pairs it with screening enforced by the contract and sends no fee to a team address. It does offer 25% of the token to the public through a pool, and offering tokens to the public is the step regulators target most directly. Those choices reduce the exposure. They do not remove it.

Founder allocation and pseudonymity. The founder's 5% sits at a public address for two years, and that address is the first thing anyone will trace. Using a fresh wallet does not make its owner anonymous: how it is funded and where its tokens are eventually sold can both be followed. The review recommended choosing one of two options, pseudonymity with no special allocation or an allocation held openly. The owner chose a pseudonymous allocation. It is recorded here as a decision, not as a recommendation, and it should be put to a lawyer before any deployment.

Cost. Three more small contracts (token, distributor with staking, vesting and builder distribution) go through the same IMD review, proofs and canary as the pool. A token is worth little until the pool has volume: Railgun's 0.25% brings in about $312,000 a month at $106M, and a pool the size of Privacy Pools v2 would bring in a few thousand.

On the jury size

The shipped code answers the first version's question. Open questions

  1. Can IMD's audit panels find the kind of bug that breaks privacy pools? Nothing in the research shows they have audited a zero-knowledge circuit. This launch is the test.
  2. Can IMD's launch factory issue a token with no owner and no mint function, and send its trade fee to a contract? If not, the distribution contract places the pool itself.
  3. Which wallet will ship the pool? Nobody has been asked.
  4. Parameters to settle before the ceremony, because they can never change: exit sizes per asset, the minimum age, each asset's share of emissions, the screener stake, and the starting caps.
  5. Will exchanges accept screening at entry, as Railgun does it, or expect screening at exit, as Privacy Pools does it?
  6. Will any wallet with real users fill its trusted-signer list from seat delegations? Until one does, the mesh relays only for the reference wallet.
  7. Can an independent operator reach a PPOI node? The broadcaster has no default URL, and no public aggregator open to third parties was found.
  8. Do Railway's iOS and Android builds enforce the allowlist? The web and desktop minimum version does. The mobile minimum predates the change.
  9. Who operates the four allowlisted fee signers and the three bootstrap nodes of Railgun's Waku cluster?
  10. Does any recipient want a second, published-criteria association proof for a Privacy Pools withdrawal? None has asked.
  11. How will Judge Failla's Rule 29 ruling and the April 2027 retrial treat independent relayers? The government's position at the April 2026 hearing was that relayers are part of the operating cohort.
  12. Does an EU seat holder who relays for a fee fall under any regulated category? AMLR Article 2(1)(25) defines anonymity-enhancing coins and includes optional privacy. Recital 160 carves out software providers without access to wallets. AMLA's guidance on self-hosted addresses is due by 10 July 2027.
  13. Is the trusted setup behind Privacy Pools v1's verifier documented well enough to reuse? This matters only if the pool option is exercised.
  14. Does Railgun adopt frame transactions or a 4337 path first, and remove the relayer before the mesh matters?
