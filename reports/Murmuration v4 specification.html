What it is

Murmuration is a privacy pool on Ethereum mainnet. You deposit ETH or a supported token, hold and send it privately inside the pool, and withdraw to any address. Its contracts have no owner, no pause and no upgrade path. The code is written and audited by the IMD swarm, a network of about 1,150 AI agents run by the holders of 2,000 seat NFTs. The same seat holders run the nodes that relay transactions, publish the pool's public data and screen deposits.

Four features define the pool.

  • Immutable and screened. Tornado Cash is immutable and unscreened, so exchanges treat its exits as tainted. Railgun and Privacy Pools are screened and keep admin keys. Murmuration is designed to be both immutable and screened.
  • Fixed-size exits on a private ledger. Inside the pool, amounts are arbitrary and transfers are private. Leaving it, funds come out only in standard sizes, so a withdrawal cannot be matched to a deposit by its amount.
  • Notes that stay private. Encrypted notes sit on-chain forever. They use a hybrid of X25519 and ML-KEM, so recording them today and decrypting them with a future quantum computer does not work.
  • Private rewards. Shielded funds earn the project's token for the time they stay shielded. The reward is collected inside the proof when a note is spent, so there is no claim to observe.

What revision 4.4 changes

On 11 November 2026 the owner went through every open default, and the question of whether to cut features or keep fixing. Revision 4.4 is revision 4.3 with those decisions applied. It changes one part of the design, how the relayer is paid, and that change is a removal.

  • The relayer is paid out of the notes being spent. The separate fee note is gone. The circuit takes two notes where it took three, and has 11 public inputs where it had 14. A relayed spend now shows which asset moved. It still hides the amount, the sender and the recipient.
  • USDT and USDC leave in sizes from 10 to 1,000,000. The page had recommended sizes from 1.
  • Every other default is confirmed as recommended. Two thirds of screeners with no fallback and removal by strikes. The stop on the caps at 800 ETH. A one-day minimum age measured at the batch. Schedules counted from the registry's first batch. Refunds by others after 21 days. A 30-day wait and 14 days of unbonding for screeners. The four-year deposit window, the floor under the reward divisor, the two-year swarm vest, and the auction with its buyback.
  • Scope stays. Asked whether to cut features or keep fixing them, the owner chose to keep every feature. Findings are answered by fixes.

Nothing is left for the owner to confirm. The decisions and the reasons are in The defaults, as decided. What revision 4.3 changed

The first version 4 was written in one session and its new parts had not been reviewed. Revision 4.1 reviewed them and redesigned what failed. Independent reviewers then found one critical and six high faults in revision 4.1, and seven high faults in revision 4.2, which was the answer to the first of those reviews. Most of the second set were in what revision 4.2 had added. Revision 4.3 answers them, and where it can it removes a mechanism instead of adding one. The owner's decisions are unchanged.

  • The relay fee has a ceiling and pays the 0.2%. In revision 4.2 it was a way to take any amount of ETH out of the pool with no fee and no standard size.
  • The relayer could be paid in any listed asset, from a separate fee note. An ETH-only fee note shut out everyone who could not get one, which is everyone new once the ETH cap is full. Revision 4.4 removes the fee note altogether.
  • The launch cannot be blocked. It tries up to 256 addresses and needs one whose market nobody has created. The price-reset code is gone.
  • A flag needs a majority and names one deposit. A third of screeners could have refused anyone.
  • Blocking earns strikes whether it is done by silence or by a wrong answer. A group could otherwise hold the registry to one batch a week for ever.
  • A batch is applied on its own day or not at all, so no batch depends on another.
  • The proofs must show that honest spends are accepted, and the list says which statements are machine-checked, which are checked on the key, and which are assumed.
  • Schedules start at the registry's first batch, and the figures on this page use the stop on the caps.

The reasons are in Review of revision 4.2. The work is broken into 50 jobs in the build plan.

Decided by the owner since version 4: exit sizes start at 0.005 ETH (10 November). IMD is a listed asset with full reward weight and a cap of its own (11 November). The asset list is ETH, USDT, USDC, IMD and the pool's own token (11 November). The screener stake is 0.03% of supply (11 November). The relayer is paid from the notes being spent, USDT and USDC leave in sizes from 10, and every remaining default is confirmed (11 November, in session).

sequenceDiagram

  1. Deposit. You send the amount you want shielded plus 0.2%, from a public address, together with a hidden commitment to the key that will own it. The deposit is public. Nothing is shielded yet.
  2. Screening. Once a day the screeners agree on a batch. It covers the deposits that were at least a day old when the day began, and says which of them are refused. A batch needs two thirds of the active screeners.
  3. Activation. When a batch covers your deposit without refusing it, you activate it from the same address, and a note of exactly the amount enters the tree. From deposit to activation takes between about two days and three when screeners do their part on time, and longer when they do not.
  4. Refund. At any time before activation you can take the deposit and its 0.2% back, to the address it came from. No screening decision can stop it. A deposit that has stayed refused for three weeks can also be sent back by anyone.
  5. Private use. An activated note is an ordinary shielded note. You can hold it, split it and pay other users. When a relayer sends the transaction, observers see that it happened and which asset moved, and nothing about who or how much.
  6. Relaying. Your wallet hands the encrypted transaction to a relayer picked at random, so your own address never pays the gas. The relayer's fee is public, like a gas payment, and is limited to a few times the cost of the gas. It is paid in the asset you are spending, out of the notes being spent, so a relayed transaction shows which asset moved and nothing else about it.
  7. Withdrawal. Funds leave to any address in standard sizes. The recipient receives the size minus 0.2%, the same amount for everyone who withdraws that size. A larger amount leaves as several standard withdrawals, spread over time by the wallet.

Assurance without an external audit

The owner's decision is to rely on IMD and see whether the swarm can secure what it builds. Wallets, L2BEAT and exchanges look for a named firm's report, so adoption will be slower without one. Four measures make the test fair and keep a failure small.

  • IMD audit panels on everything. Every contract and the circuit go through IMD's audit jobs, with each finding and its fix public. Reviewers built on the same models as the builders tend to share blind spots, which is why the next three measures do not depend on reviewers.
  • Machine-checked proofs. The swarm writes formal proofs of the relation and the eleven lemmas listed under the circuit, one of which is that honest spends are accepted. Anyone can re-run the checker. A checked proof holds whoever wrote it, as long as the statement it proves is the right one, so each statement is also published in plain words for review. The first two lists of statements were too weak. Reviewers showed that a broken circuit could have passed the first, and a circuit that accepts nothing the second. Open-source analysers for under-constrained circuits are run in public as well.
  • A canary. The whole system is deployed on mainnet 90 days before launch with a compressed clock and small caps, funded by the project and exercised by a published script. It is a rehearsal and a standing reward for anyone who can break it. It does not prove the circuit sound: someone who finds a flaw can wait for the larger pool.
  • Low caps. The pool opens at 25 ETH, 50,000 of each stablecoin and 2,500 IMD and doubles every 90 days. The most that a circuit flaw can take is about $160,000 at launch and about $5.1M at the stop the owner confirmed. Without a stop the same schedule reaches about $41M after two years.

Nothing in the research shows that IMD has audited a zero-knowledge circuit before. This launch is that test.

Screening

A screening batch is the output of a deterministic program. Its inputs are a published criteria file, finalized chain data up to the start of the day, list files pinned by hash, and flags that screeners have posted on-chain. Any node can run it, and two honest nodes with the same data get the same batch. Each screener commits to a hash of its batch and reveals it later the same day. The registry accepts a batch when two thirds of the active screeners have revealed the same one, and anyone then applies it in the last four hours of the day.

Two limits of this example are worth saying plainly. The funding rules see only transfers that appear in a log or as the value of a top-level transaction. ETH moved inside a contract call is invisible to them, so one hop through a contract avoids both. They catch the careless. And anyone can send back a refused deposit once it is 21 days old, so the node software does not signal a file in which a hold runs past a deposit age of 14 days.

  • Who screens. Anyone who holds an IMD seat and stakes the token can register. Thirty days later the screener asks to be counted, and counts from the next day. There is no curator and no allowlist.
  • What it decides. Whether a deposit may be activated. It never decides whether the depositor keeps the money: a deposit can be refunded to its sender at any time before activation.
  • When. At entry. A batch covers only deposits that were already a day old when its day began, so whatever a screener learns in the first day after a deposit can still stop it. That holds from the first batch on, because seats can flag while they wait to become active. A refusal can be added or lifted until the moment of activation. After activation a note carries no label, so private transfers need no screening proof.
  • How fresh information gets in. A list cannot know about a theft from this morning. A registered screener can post a flag on-chain against a deposit. Under the published rule, flags from a fifth of the screeners, and at least three, hold the deposit until it is eight days old, and flags from more than half refuse it. A flag counts for two weeks and can be posted again.
  • What a flag quorum costs. Whatever number of seats can cause a refusal controls who may enter. Revision 4.2 let a third refuse, and three seats in a small registry. Revision 4.3 asked for more than half of all seats, and a reviewer showed the other side of that: seats that only run the program never flag, so a theft that is on no list got through unless half of all seats acted by hand inside two days. The example now has two steps. A fifth can delay any deposit by about a week, once. Only a majority can refuse. The price is that a fifth of the seats can make every depositor wait eight days. The file is not in any contract, so screeners can adopt another.
  • Changing the criteria. Lists go stale, so criteria files are versioned. Nodes adopt a new file when more than two thirds of the screeners have signalled its hash, and keep it until another file reaches two thirds. Nobody coordinates this: it is a rule in the node software, and the registry sees only batch hashes.
  • Getting the same answer. The example file uses only logs, receipts and the value of top-level transactions, which every node can check against block headers. It leaves out transfers made inside contract calls, which data providers report differently. Nodes run published test vectors before they commit. A screener whose data differs from the majority's gains strikes exactly as a blocker does.
  • How screeners are paid. 40% of the pool's fee and 15% of the token, split equally among the screeners who revealed that day's batch, on days when the batch was accepted and applied. A day without a batch pays nothing and its fees wait for the next one. Never per withdrawal, because that would reward approving more.
  • If screeners disappear. Anyone can remove a screener that has not revealed for seven days. If fewer than seven remain, or no batch reaches two thirds, new deposits wait or are refunded. Everything already in the pool keeps working. With exactly seven, one departure stops activation until a replacement has waited its 30 days. In a registry of seven to ten, removing a blocking group has the same effect, so the honest screeners there choose between a registry that is blocked some days and one that is stopped for a month.
  • If a minority blocks. A group of more than a third can stop a day's batch by not revealing it. A member gains three strikes for each day it fails to reveal the majority's batch, and loses one for each day it reveals a batch that is accepted. At 21 strikes the seat is removed and cannot register again for 90 days, and the strikes follow the seat if it is sold. A group can block for seven to ten days at a stretch and after that for at most about one day in three. With screeners split exactly in half nothing is accepted and nobody is penalized.
  • If screeners are bought. Whoever holds a majority can have any deposit refused, by flagging it, and after a week can remove the other screeners. Whoever holds two thirds can also approve any deposit that is at least a day old. None of them can move funds, and anyone can recompute the batch and see what they did. Refusing others matters because rewards go to whoever is inside: control of the registry is also control of who earns the users' 45%.

What screening at entry does not do. It stops funds that a list or a flag identifies within a day or two of the deposit. Stolen funds that nobody has identified by then enter and cannot be removed afterwards. Railgun's check has the same limit with a one-hour wait. Privacy Pools can still exclude a deposit until it is withdrawn, and is stronger on this point. The criteria are public, so a careful thief can test a deposit against them in advance.

What the registry does not prove. It is as independent as the seats are: one holder with many seats is many screeners, and one address holds 77 of the 2,000. Commit and reveal stops a screener copying another's answer from the chain. It cannot stop screeners sharing an answer off-chain, so agreement may reflect fewer computations than signatures. And a data provider that serves most of the screeners decides what the majority sees, with no seat at all.

Why screen at all

A pool that accepts every deposit is possible. Tornado Cash is one, and what happened to it is the reason this pool does not copy it.

  • Thieves arrive first. A Cambridge study found attackers used Tornado Cash in 78% of Ethereum incidents through mid-2025. The wallet behind the Binance hack of October 2026 was funded through it.
  • Honest users pay for it. Exchanges flag or freeze funds that come out of an unscreened pool, and some block accounts with only indirect exposure. A private withdrawal nobody will accept is worth little.
  • Issuers can freeze the pool. Circle froze the USDC inside Tornado Cash's pool in 2022. A pool known to hold stolen funds invites the same.
  • The fee depends on it. This pool takes a fee and has a token. Profiting from every deposit, stolen ones included, is the pattern the Tornado Cash and Samourai prosecutions turned on. Railgun and 0xbow charge fees, screen, and have not been charged.
  • Wallets are less likely to offer it otherwise. People reach a pool through a wallet app, and the launch gate requires one with real users. Wallet teams carry their own legal and app-store exposure, and the wallets that offer privacy today, Railway and Ambire, chose a screened pool. This is not absolute: the Ethereum Foundation's Kohaku kit has a Tornado Cash plugin in alpha.
  • There would be no reason to exist. An unscreened immutable pool already exists and holds about $780M. A new one would be the same thing with no crowd.

Screening does not weaken an honest user's privacy. It looks only at the deposit, which is public in any pool, and after activation nothing connects the deposit to what happens inside. What it costs is a wait of two to three days.

Before the pool exists, the same screening program serves as a second opinion on Privacy Pools v1 withdrawals. The user makes a side proof with Privacy Pools' own circuit, and anyone checks it against the verifier contract already deployed there. That is how screeners build a public record first.

Token and fee

The first two versions of this spec forbade a protocol fee and a token. On 10 November 2026 the owner decided to add both, to keep the pool fully immutable, and to keep the project decentralized. This section gives them the only shape that fits those three decisions, and says plainly what they change.

The fee

  • 0.2% on the way in and 0.2% on the way out, taken in the asset itself. Together that is 0.4%, against Railgun's 0.5%. Transfers inside the pool are free.
  • The entry fee is paid on top of the deposit and taken when the deposit is activated, so a refunded deposit is returned with its fee. The exit fee comes out of the standard size. The relayer's fee leaves the pool too and pays the same 0.2%.
  • The rate and the destination are fixed in code. Nobody can raise, lower or redirect it.
  • It goes to an immutable distributor: 40% to token stakers in proportion to stake, 40% split equally among the screeners who revealed that day's batch, when it was accepted and applied, and 20% to buy the token from its locked pool.
  • Stakers are paid in the asset the fee was taken in, evenly over the seven days after each sweep, so staking for one block around a sweep earns nothing extra. A later sweep spreads what is left together with the new amount.
  • The screeners' 40% goes to the registry while it is working, which means it has applied a batch within the last seven days and still takes commits. It is paid out at the next applied batch. Otherwise the 40% goes to stakers. A day without a batch therefore delays the screeners' pay and removes none of it. The penalty for blocking a batch is strikes and removal, not lost fees: sending a blocked day's fees to stakers would pay whoever holds both seats and stake for blocking.

The public pool

  • The launch transaction creates the token, then places 25% of supply as one-sided liquidity in a Uniswap v3 pool against IMD, at the 1% fee tier. Buyers bring IMD and the price rises as they buy. There is no ETH pair.
  • The range starts at a valuation of 48,000 IMD for the whole supply, about $127,000 with IMD at $2.64, and runs upward without a practical limit so the pool never runs out of tokens to buy. With a share f of the pool's tokens bought, the pool holds 12,000 × f / (1 − f) IMD and the price is the start divided by (1 − f)². At half bought that is 12,000 IMD and four times the start. The simulation tabulates it.
  • A one-sided pool starts with no IMD in it, so sellers can only sell into what buyers have paid, and below the starting price there are no bids at all.
  • Anyone can create and price a Uniswap pool for an address that has no code yet. Revision 4.1 let the launch revert if someone had done that. Revision 4.2 made the address depend on the launch block, and a reviewer showed that anyone ordered ahead of the launch in that block could still read it and make the launch revert. The launch now tries up to 256 addresses and takes the first whose pool does not exist. A pool costs about 4.5M gas to create, so nobody can cover them all in one block, and the candidates change with every block.
  • The token's address is always below IMD's, so the pool has one orientation and every formula on this page is the one that runs.
  • Whoever sends the launch transaction can also buy first, at the bottom of the range. Buying a quarter of the pool's tokens there costs about 4,000 IMD, roughly $10,600. A permissionless launch cannot prevent this.
  • Until rewards and vesting put tokens into circulation, this pool is the only place to buy a screener's stake. The owner has set the stake at 0.03% of supply. The first then costs about 14 IMD, the 100th about 19 and the 500th about 90, and the pool holds enough tokens for 833 stakes. Revision 4.3 had recommended 0.005%, so that every seat could stake from the pool. At the 0.05% of revision 4.2 the 500th could not have been bought at all.
  • The liquidity position is held by the distribution contract, which has no code path that withdraws it. The trading fees it earns are collected by anyone: the IMD part joins the buyback below and the token part is burned. Uniswap's protocol takes a share first. On pools at this fee tier it has been one sixth of the fees since February 2026, anyone can switch it on for a new pool, and for every recent pool someone did within half an hour. Uniswap's governance can raise it to a quarter. A trader pays the same 1% either way.
  • IMD's launch factory was checked and is not used. It can issue a token with no owner and no mint, and its trading fee can go to a contract. But only IMD's own key can call it, it always gives 10% of supply to seat holders, and it would hold the position itself.
  • It is a market and a tie to IMD. It raises no money for the project: what buyers pay stays in the pool.
  • It is priced in IMD, a thin and volatile token (about $1.14M in its main pool, which is not locked, and an 18% move in two days this week). IMD has no mint function and a fixed supply of about 7 million across Ethereum and Base. On Ethereum it is a bridged token, and one owner key decides which bridge peers may credit it, so that key is trusted not to create IMD there. If it did, it could also buy every fee basket with it. If IMD fails, this liquidity fails with it.

The 20%: auction and buyback

  1. The distributor releases 20% of every fee receipt into a basket evenly over seven days, so the basket's value never jumps. For fees paid in the pool's own token, the 20% is burned directly. For fees paid in IMD, it goes straight to the buyback in step 3.
  2. One continuous descending-price auction sells the whole basket for IMD. The price falls in a straight line from a starting price to a floor over one day and waits at the floor. Whoever accepts the price first pays it in IMD and takes the assets they name. The next starting price is twice the price just paid. The first sale's clock starts when the first fees arrive.
  3. The IMD is spent buying the token from the locked pool, one step every 12 hours, and the bought tokens are burned.
  4. Each step is at most half of the pool's fee rate times the locked position's virtual IMD reserve, measured from the position's own fixed liquidity and never from a balance someone could inflate. A step of the full product is the size at which a sandwich starts to pay, so a whole day's buying stays at that line. The reserve is taken at the current price or the starting price, whichever is higher, so that parking the price at the bottom of the range cannot shrink a step to nothing.

The owner's decision was 20% into locked liquidity. This does not add liquidity in the technical sense: it moves the price up the locked position's curve and burns what it buys. The IMD ends up in the pool as bids. It stays there as long as the locked position is the liquidity in range. If someone else adds liquidity at the current price, part of each step is bought from them and that part of the IMD leaves with them, in exchange for tokens that are burned all the same. A holder can do this on purpose for the length of one transaction and sell nearly a whole step's worth of their own tokens. The bids that do remain are there for any seller to take. And while the market trades below the starting price on other people's liquidity, each step pays the starting price or more for most of what it buys.

Version 4 added the IMD as one-sided liquidity at the current price, which the buyer of the auction could set and then sell into. The buyback reaches nearly the same end without that opening. No price feed and no key is involved in any step. Anyone who sees IMD waiting for the buyback can buy ahead of it and sell later. That is ordinary speculation with price risk, and the slow pace is what keeps it from being a sure thing.

How private rewards work

  1. The pool keeps one public number, a reward index, which grows every second by that second's emission divided by the weighted total shielded. Each asset's weight is fixed in the build, because an immutable pool has no price oracle. The divisor never falls below a quarter of what the pool can hold, so a nearly empty pool cannot pay a huge rate.
  2. A note records, hidden inside its commitment, the index value when it was created. For a deposit the pool writes the exact value at activation. For notes made by a spend, the proof uses a checkpoint that anyone can refresh once an hour.
  3. When the note is spent, the proof computes what it earned: its value times its asset's weight times how far the index has moved. The amount is carried into the new notes as a hidden field.
  4. When the owner wants the tokens, any spend can turn the carried amount into a token note. Every spend has that output slot whether it is used or not, so nobody can tell a claim from a payment. Tornado's reward scheme leaked deposit age at exactly that step. Taking the tokens out of the pool is a public withdrawal like any other, and its size shows that the holder had earned at least that much.

A reward follows value multiplied by time, so splitting a deposit gains nothing. Checkpoints always round against the spender, so the total paid can never exceed the emission, which the independent review checked and confirmed. The token has its own withdrawal limit inside the pool, so a mistake in the reward logic can at worst drain the reward budget released so far and any tokens users have deposited, and never another asset.

Rewards exist to gather a first crowd and to spread the token to real users. They do not keep users. The evidence is that people who come for yield leave when it ends, so the product has to earn the rest. Emissions run 730 days from the registry's first batch and follow the caps. Otherwise most of the users' share would go to whoever filled a 25 ETH pool in its first months. Whoever fills a cap early still collects that step's rewards. It costs them the 0.2% on the way in and the 0.2% on the way out, and less for anyone who holds much of the stake or many screener seats, since 80% of every fee goes back to stakers and screeners. At the starting valuation the reward is small: about 1.1% a year on a full pool with the stop on the caps, and four times that on a pool a quarter full or less.

IMD is listed with full reward weight: at the prices on the day the parameters are frozen, an IMD note earns the same per dollar as an ETH note. Its cap is small, 2,500 IMD rising to 80,000, so a full IMD cap earns about 4% of emissions when the pool is full and at most about 17% while the other assets are nearly empty. USDT and USDC are weighted at what a dollar is worth in ETH on the same day. Every weight is a fixed exchange rate and will drift from the market, and all are frozen before the ceremony, months before launch.

What this changes

Legal position. A fee and a token make the project look like a business to financial-crime and securities enforcement. In the Tornado Cash and Samourai prosecutions, the money the developers made was part of the case. Railgun and 0xbow charge fees and have not been charged; both pair the fee with real screening. This design pairs it with screening enforced by the contract and sends no fee to a team address. It does offer 25% of the token to the public through a pool, and offering tokens to the public is the step regulators target most directly. Stakers receive a share of fees, which is the feature securities law looks at first. Those choices reduce the exposure in some places and add to it in others. They do not remove it.

Founder allocation and pseudonymity. The founder's 5% sits at a public address for two years. The review recommended choosing one of two options, pseudonymity with no special allocation or an allocation held openly. The owner chose a pseudonymous allocation. It is recorded here as a decision, not as a recommendation, and it should be put to a lawyer before any deployment.

This is not legal advice. A lawyer reviews the fee, the token, the public pool and the founder allocation before launch, and before the canary, which places a token pool of its own.

Cost. The token contracts go through the same IMD review, proofs and canary as the pool. A token is worth little until the pool has volume: Railgun's 0.25% brings in about $312,000 a month at $106M, and the caps hold this pool's fee income under about $400 a month for its first 90 days (see the simulation).

The questions this review had to answer

Can a thief get a deposit approved, spend once, and be untouchable?

Yes, if nothing public marks the funds as stolen before the deposit matures. Version 4 gave screeners "about a day" and a deterministic program over pinned lists. A fresh theft is on no list after a day: sanctions designations take weeks. The criteria are public, so the thief can run the screener before depositing and add hops until it passes. After the first spend nothing can be undone.

This is the same guarantee Railgun's one-hour check gives, with a longer wait. It is weaker than Privacy Pools, where a deposit can be removed from the approved set until it is withdrawn. Version 4 was unclear on one point that made it worse: it accepted any past root, so a deposit approved once stayed spendable after a later root dropped it.

What the build specification does about it. Screeners can post flags on-chain, and the published rule turns flags into refusals, so a human or an agent who learns of a theft has a way to act inside a deterministic system. A refusal applies until the moment of activation, and only the current screening state counts. The criteria can hold large or freshly funded deposits longer than the one-day floor without any contract change. What remains is stated plainly in the risks: screening at entry stops funds that are identifiable in time, and nothing else.

Can someone stall screeners for seven days to trigger the fallback?

Yes, and it is the cheapest attack in version 4. The threshold was two thirds of registered screeners, so anyone holding more than one third blocks every root by doing nothing. With 15 honest screeners that is 8 seats: about 5.6 ETH of capital that can be resold afterwards, plus 8 stakes that are returned. After seven days the fallback approves any deposit whose sender is not on the sanctions oracle, which is every thief who is not already sanctioned. The same thing happens without an attacker if a third of registered screeners simply stop.

The fallback also tied a contract that can never change to the Chainalysis oracle, a contract whose owner can change or retire it.

The build specification removes the fallback. If no batch is accepted, new deposits wait or go back to their senders, and everything already in the pool keeps working. A third of screeners can no longer switch screening off. Revision 4.1 said that blocking deposits earned them nothing and that the pool recovered from attrition by itself. Both were wrong: blockers already inside the pool keep the rewards to themselves, and someone has to call the removal. Revision 4.2 let anyone remove a screener after seven days of silence or seven days running against the majority. A reviewer then showed how to block for ever without doing either, and revision 4.3 counts strikes instead. This changes an unconfirmed default and is listed for the owner.

The reward index

  • It could pay out more than the budget. With a daily step, a note created just before the update and spent just after earns a full day for a few minutes inside. Value that cycles in and out around each update collects a day's reward per cycle, and the total issued exceeds what was emitted. The token's own withdrawal limit would then stop the last claimants from leaving.
  • The first depositor. The index grew by emission divided by the total shielded. With 0.001 ETH in an otherwise empty asset, one note collects that asset's whole emission, at a nominal rate above 40,000% a year (see the simulation). With zero shielded the division fails outright.
  • A proof cannot follow a moving number. The index a proof was built against has changed by the time a relayer lands the transaction.
  • A per-asset index names the asset. A private transfer would have to say which asset's index it used, or carry every asset's index as a public input.
  • Reward dust. A new reward note on every spend leaves an active wallet with hundreds of tiny notes that each cost a transfer to merge.

The build specification uses one index for all assets with fixed weights. A deposit takes the exact index at the moment it is activated, computed by the contract. Spends use a checkpoint that anyone can roll forward once an hour, which always rounds against the spender, so the total issued can never exceed the emission. The divisor has a floor of a quarter of capacity, which caps the rate at four times the full-pool rate and leaves the rest of the emission locked for good. The reward is carried inside the note and turned into a token note only when the owner chooses, inside an ordinary spend that looks like any other.

Fixed-size exits: change, dust, and a large withdrawal

  • Change stays inside as a new note. That part works.
  • Dust below the smallest size can never leave by itself. Once deposits close it can never be topped up either. The owner has set the smallest size at 0.005 ETH, about $11, and it is 1 unit for a stablecoin. A remainder under that can leave only after it is merged with another note.
  • Arbitrary deposits undo part of the benefit. A deposit of 7.3134 ETH followed by exits of 5, 2, 0.2 and 0.1 to one address shows the total to anyone who adds them up. Fixed sizes stop exact matching of a single withdrawal. They do not hide a total sent to one place. The wallet should deposit in standard sizes by default and warn before sending several exits to one address.
  • A large withdrawal is a visible run of the largest size. Ten exits of 100 ETH in a week in a pool capped at 800 ETH are one actor, whatever the proofs hide.
  • A public relay fee would mark each exit. If the relayer's fee came out of the withdrawn amount, payouts of the same size would differ by the fee quoted. Revision 4.1 paid relayers with a shielded note. Revision 4.2 pays them a public amount of ETH out of a separate shielded ETH note, because collecting shielded fee notes would have doubled the gas a user pays for. Revision 4.3 let the fee be paid in any listed asset and put a ceiling on it. Revision 4.4 pays it out of the notes being spent, as a second public amount beside the withdrawal. In every version the fee never comes out of the withdrawn amount, so every exit of a given size pays out the same amount to the wei.

The daily auction

  • No first price. With no oracle, the first auction has nothing to start from. If it starts low, the first bot takes the basket for almost nothing.
  • Zero-bid days. At $1M a month the 20% share is about $13 a day. When gas is high it is not worth collecting, and a rule that resets daily would give it away at the floor.
  • One frozen asset blocks the rest. If a stablecoin issuer freezes the distributor, a settlement that transfers every asset reverts for all of them.
  • The liquidity add is the real hole, as described in the table above.

The build specification keeps the owner's mechanism and changes how each step is done. One continuous descending-price auction sells the whole basket for IMD. After a sale the next starting price is twice the price just paid, with a floor, and the buyer names which assets to take so a frozen one can be skipped. The IMD is then spent buying the token from the locked pool in small steps and the bought tokens are burned. The locked position can never be withdrawn, so its share of that IMD stays in the pool for good, which is what "into locked liquidity" asked for. A step smaller than the pool's fee rate times its IMD reserves cannot be sandwiched at a profit. Revision 4.2 limits each step to half of that, measured from the locked position's own liquidity so that nobody can inflate the figure, and allows one step every 12 hours. The bound is checked numerically in reports/sim/buyback_sandwich_bound.py.

Screener registration

  • Cost to approve a bad deposit: two thirds of active screeners. With 15 honest ones that is 30 seats, about 21 ETH or $45,000 of resalable capital. One address already holds 77 seats and needs to buy nothing. The independent review showed the prize is larger than one bad deposit: two thirds decide who may enter at all, and so who earns the rewards.
  • Cost to block: one third, 8 seats. Under version 4 that also meant switching screening off. Without the fallback it only delays deposits.
  • The prize grows and the cost does not. The cap doubles every 90 days. The price of 30 seats does not. After a year the cap on ETH is 400 ETH, with the other assets in step, and buying the registry to launder into it pays for itself.
  • Pay per head invites one holder to register every seat. Each registered seat earns a full share, so the largest seat holders end up with the largest share of the registry. The top ten addresses hold 382 of the 2,000 seats. Counting holders does not help, because a seat moves to a fresh wallet for the price of a transfer.
  • Stake and exit. Version 4 had no waiting period. Seats could be bought, registered, used to approve one deposit and sold in the same week.

The build specification adds a 30-day wait before a new screener counts, a 14-day wait to take the stake back, a commit and reveal so that a batch cannot be copied from the chain, individual reveals in place of signatures gathered by one submitter, and a minimum of seven active screeners before any batch can be accepted. Screeners can still share a batch privately, and a two-thirds bloc can still agree on one the others did not compute. It does not solve concentration. Nothing on-chain can tell one holder with many seats from many holders, and the spec now says so.

Does the canary exercise the same code paths?

No, not as version 4 described it. Ninety days with 10 ETH never reaches the second cap step, the end of emissions, the end of the deposit window, a second asset, a refused deposit, the auction or the buyback. It also needs its own token and its own public pool to run the token paths at all, which makes the canary a small public token offering and puts the lawyer's review before the canary, not after it.

Silence proves less than it seems. Someone who finds a flaw in the canary can take 10 ETH now or wait for the same flaw in a pool that holds far more. The canary catches the impatient.

The build specification makes the canary the complete system from the same source, with the clock compressed: ten-day cap steps and small caps, so every schedule boundary and the close of deposits pass on mainnet inside the 90 days. It runs a published script with a checklist of functions and spend types, and the paths that cannot be reached on mainnet in that time are run on a fork. It is described as a rehearsal that catches deployment and integration errors and careless attackers. The caps remain what limits a loss.

Per-asset limits and the cap schedule

  • What the limit bounds. A fault in one asset, such as a token that starts charging a transfer fee, cannot drain another asset. A fault in the reward logic cannot pay out more than the rewards released so far.
  • What it does not bound. A flaw in the circuit forges notes in every asset at once. The loss is every asset's balance, so the figure to watch is the cap multiplied by the number of assets.
  • The schedule has no end. Version 4 said the cap reaches "about 6,400 ETH after two years" and never said it stops. At that level the pool could hold about $41M in code no outside firm has read. Two more years of doubling would remove the cap in all but name.
  • Where the cap is counted. The build specification counts it on activated value only. Unactivated deposits cannot be reached by any proof, so they need no cap, and counting them would let a free refundable deposit hold the pool shut.

Other changes that came out of it

  • The relayer is paid in ETH. A fee paid as a shielded note cost the relayer about one more transaction to collect, so users would have paid twice the gas, and the relayer learned the asset. A small shielded ETH note now pays a public fee.

  • Exits cannot be blocked by filling the root window with empty spends: the root saved at each checkpoint is accepted too.

  • Blocking "earns the blockers nothing". It earned them the rewards if they were already inside.
  • "The pool recovers from ordinary attrition by itself." Someone has to call evict.
  • Capture means "a bad deposit approved". It means control of entry.
  • "One to two days for screeners." It was zero to one.
  • "The launch code handles a pool that already exists." It could be blocked.
  • "That IMD stays in the pool as bids for good." Only for the locked position's share of the liquidity in range.
  • The canary "calls every function and every branch". Its full-cap line could not be reached as funded.

Not adopted

  • Refusals that expire unless renewed, to make refusing everyone costly. A bloc that controls the registry can renew them.
  • A launch that never reverts, selling into any bids it finds. Unbounded, it would hand the whole public allocation to whoever parked bids just above the start.
  • A third ciphertext for the relayer. Paying the relayer in ETH removes the need.

What revision 4.3 takes out

  • Any link between one day's batch and another's. There is no order of application to get wrong.
  • The pot that carried blocked days forward to whoever revealed next.
  • The count of consecutive misses, replaced by one counter that also covers silence.
  • The fixed start date for caps and emissions, replaced by the first batch.
  • The claim that addresses of one wallet cannot be linked.

  • A public fee asset and a fee ceiling that reads the block's base fee.
  • A path for value between the fee side and the asset side of a spend when both are the same asset.
  • Credits for ETH payments that fail, and a function to claim them.
  • The strike counter and the 90-day bar.
  • A start time read from the registry by the pool.
  • A salt loop in the launch.

Claims in revision 4.2 that were wrong

  • "Only someone building that same block can still interfere" with the launch. Anyone ordered ahead of it could.
  • "A third of the screeners can delay deposits for about a week and no longer." They could do it for ever.
  • "Three seats cannot have an exchange's customers refused." Three seats could have anyone refused.
  • "The buyback … cannot be sandwiched at a profit." True inside the locked range and not below it.
  • Funds leave "only in standard sizes". Not through the relay fee as it was.

  • "The wallet keeps a fee note topped up", rated low. Many users could not have had one.
  • The reward and unlock figures were for a schedule with no stop on the caps, while the build was written to the stop.

Not fixed, and why

  • Seven screeners is a cliff (M-24). One departure at seven stops activation for 30 days. A shorter wait at low numbers is the shortcut a large seat holder would use.
  • A frozen depositor's deposit stays in the pool (M-20). Nothing in an immutable pool can send a token to an address its issuer has frozen.
  • Insiders pay a fifth of the fee (M-26), and one honest seat earns about $2 a day (M-15). Both follow from the fee split, which is decided.
  • The float limits the registry (M-15). At the owner's stake of 0.03% the pool can supply 833 stakes, and early buyers raise the price for later ones.
  • IMD's bridge key (M-25) could buy the fee baskets. The pairing with IMD is decided.
  • A data provider can steer the majority (M-16). Reduced by limiting the criteria to data nodes can check, not removed.
  • The buyback below the starting price (M-8, M-21). It overpays there, and a holder can sell into it. Stated in the risks.
  • Two suggested mechanisms were not taken: a second bitmap for holds, and batches split into chained chunks. The age rule on refunds and the rule that refusals are listed first do the same work with less.

What held

Total reward that can be claimed never exceeds what was released. The pool's ledgers close for every call. No capture of the registry can move funds, stop a depositor's own refund, or approve a deposit less than a day and a half old. The auction, which follows Euler's audited design, showed no profitable manipulation. The Deployer is not circular. The range, cap, fee and reward arithmetic and the gas arithmetic could be redone from the page. The sandwich bound holds inside the locked range even when the step is sized after the attacker moves.

  • accrued is reward already earned and not yet turned into tokens. It travels with the note. index is the value of g the note was created against.
  • A wallet is rebuilt from its seed and the chain. This is a rule for the wallet, not a suggestion. It finds its deposits by trying counters against Deposited events and stops after 20 counters in a row with no match. It finds its own spends by trying k1 on every Spent event, and payments to it by trying its X-Wing key on ciphertext 0. Values and the asset come from the ciphertexts and the index from gRef in the event. It counts a payment only after it has recomputed the commitment from the decrypted payload, gRef and its own ownerPk, and found it equal to C[0] of the same event. Someone else may copy an ownerCommit into a deposit of their own, so several deposits can carry one ownerCommit, and the wallet offers to activate or refund only deposits made from its own addresses. Two devices that share a seed check the chain for the next free counter just before each deposit. If both deposit at the same moment the two deposits carry one ownerCommit and can be seen to belong together.
  • Empty inputs and unused outputs. An empty input has value and accrued zero and index equal to gRef. Its rho, leafIndex, slot, others and path are fresh random numbers every time. They are never derived from the seed, because a repeated dummy nullifier would make a restored wallet's spend fail. An output the sender has no use for is an empty note of the sender's own, with a derived rho like any output it keeps. No output ever has a constant ownerCommit, and ciphertext 0 is always a real encryption: to the recipient, or to the sender's own key when the sender keeps output 0. Otherwise anyone could tell a claim from a payment and read the asset of a spend that shows none.
  • A view-only wallet holds viewRoot, spk and nk. It sees every note, computes every nullifier and cannot spend.
  • A wallet has one address. ownerPk is the same for all of a wallet's notes, and the address also carries the encryption key. Two people who compare what they were given can tell that it is one wallet. Revision 4.4 had a diversifier that varied ownerPk from note to note. It hid nothing on-chain that rho does not hide, a payer had no way to know it, and it is gone. A user who wants separate identities uses separate seeds.
  • The short address is ownerPk and keccak256(key). It works only after the key has been published with Pool.announce, which is an ordinary transaction from a public account. Anyone who is given the short address can then tie the wallet to that account. The wallet uses the long address unless the user chooses otherwise.
  • Ciphertext 0, 1,216 bytes, is for the recipient of output 0: HPKE base mode with the X-Wing hybrid of X25519 and ML-KEM-768 as the key encapsulation (identifier 0x647A, 1,120 bytes), HKDF-SHA256 and ChaCha20-Poly1305, with the info string "murmuration/v1/ct0" followed by the chain id and the pool's address. Then an 80-byte payload and a 16-byte tag. The payload is asset (1 byte), value (12), accrued (12), rho (31) and a memo (24). X-Wing is an individual Internet-Draft. It is not an RFC and no IETF group has adopted it. The build pins draft 11 of 23 September 2026, which was still the latest on 11 November, and its test vectors.
  • Ciphertext 1, 96 bytes, is the sender's own record under k1 with ChaCha20-Poly1305 and a zero nonce: the asset (1 byte), the value and accrued of output 1 (12 and 12), the claim in output 2 (12), and 43 spare bytes that are zero, with a 16-byte tag. The length stays at 96 bytes so that every spend publishes the same amount of data as before. The key depends on the two commitments, so a second attempt with a different fee or amount is sealed under a different key.
  • The pool checks the two lengths and nothing else about the ciphertexts. Every spend publishes exactly 1,312 bytes. A sender who posts garbage loses the payment for the recipient and gains nothing. This is the same in every shielded pool.
  • Not post-quantum: the Groth16 proof. Ownership and nullifiers rest on hashes only.

The circuit

There is one circuit, Spend. It takes two notes of one asset with one owner. It produces two notes of that asset, one note of the reward token, and two optional public amounts in that asset: an amount that leaves the pool, and a fee for the relayer. A private transfer has a public amount of zero. A withdrawal has a standard size as its public amount. A deposit needs no proof. Estimated size is about 21,000 constraints with circomlib's Poseidon, to be measured by the build job, as is proving time on a mid-range phone. The exporter of the proof framework writes more rows per hash, so the system that goes to the ceremony may be about twice that.

The relayer is paid out of the notes being spent, by the owner's decision of 11 November 2026. There is no separate fee note. A spend that pays a relayer therefore shows which asset moved and nothing else about it: not the amount moved, the sender or the recipient. A spend that its owner sends from a public address pays no relay fee and shows no asset.

Constraints

  1. Keys. spk = H2(TAG_SPK, sk), nk = H2(TAG_NK, sk) and ownerPk = H3(TAG_PK, spk, nk).
  2. Asset. asset is one of the ids 1 to 5, and weight is its constant from the table.
  3. Ranges. Every value, accrued, claim, earned, publicAmount and relayFee fits in 96 bits. gRef and each input's index fit in 90 bits. The division remainder in constraint 9 fits in 64 bits. Each leafIndex is given as 32 bits and each slot as 2 bits, and those same bits are the selectors used in constraint 5. The range checks on publicAmount and relayFee are never removed as redundant, for the reason given under lemma 10.
  4. Input notes. For each input, ownerCommit = H3(TAG_OC, ownerPk, rho) and commitment = H3(packed, index, ownerCommit), where packed is built from asset and the input's value and accrued.
  5. Membership. For each input, the commitment placed at slot among others hashes with H4 to a leaf, and that leaf, with the bits of leafIndex choosing left or right at each of the 32 levels of path, hashes to root. The equality with root is enforced unless the input is empty.
  6. Nullifiers. nf[i] = H4(TAG_NF, nk, 4 × leafIndex + slot, rho) for both inputs, from the same leafIndex, slot and rho used above, whether or not the input is empty.
  7. Distinct inputs. If both inputs are non-empty, they have different positions.
  8. Index order. For each input, gRef − index fits in 90 bits, so it is not negative.
  9. Reward earned. weight × (value₀ × (gRef − index₀) + value₁ × (gRef − index₁)) = earned × 2^64 + remainder.
  10. Outputs. C[k] = H3(packed(asset, valueₖ, accruedₖ), gRef, ownerCommitₖ) for k in 0 and 1.
  11. Reward note. C[2] = H3(packed(TOKEN, claim, 0), gRef, ownerCommit₂).
  12. Value is conserved. The two input values equal the two output values plus publicAmount plus relayFee.
  13. Reward is conserved. The two input accrued amounts plus earned equal the two output accrued amounts plus claim.
  14. The public asset is the real one. (publicAmount + relayFee) × (asset − publicAsset) = 0.
  15. Binding. extHash takes part in one constraint so that no tool can drop it from the proof.

What the circuit leaves to the contract: that every public input is below p, that the root and gRef belong to one of the last two checkpoints, that the two nullifiers are new and different, that the deadline has not passed, that the public amount is zero or a standard size, that the relay fee is under its ceiling, that the asset's balance covers what leaves, that the zero conventions in the table hold, and that extHash and the ciphertext lengths match the call.

What must be shown, and how

Revision 4.2 put everything under "machine-checked proofs". The review showed that some of its statements are not properties of a circuit at all, and that a circuit which accepts nothing satisfied every one of them. The list is now in four parts, and each part says how it is checked.

A. Machine-checked on the constraint system that goes into the ceremony. The relation theorem: every assignment that satisfies the constraints satisfies the fifteen constraints above as written in the formal specification, over the integers where they speak of sums. Then eleven lemmas about that relation, each published in plain words beside the formal statement.

  1. Spend authority. Each non-empty input's ownerCommit is built from the ownerPk that is derived from the one sk in the witness.
  2. Membership. Each non-empty input, in the sense defined above, is a note in the tree under root at the position the proof uses.
  3. One nullifier per note. A nullifier is a function of the note's position, of the rho inside its ownerCommit and of the nk inside its ownerPk, and of nothing else. Under one root, and assuming no hash collision, two satisfying assignments that spend the same position publish the same nullifier. A non-empty input always publishes its nullifier.
  4. No value is created. Both inputs and outputs 0 and 1 carry asset. No position is used twice in one proof. Over the integers, the input values equal the output values plus the public amount plus the relay fee.
  5. No reward is created. Reward leaving equals reward entering plus earned. earned uses each input's committed index and the weight of asset. Output 2 is a token note with no accrued reward.
  6. Outputs start at the checkpoint. Every output's index is gRef.
  7. The public asset is true whenever the public amount or the relay fee is non-zero.
  8. Nothing is left free. Given the private inputs and gRef, the values nf[0..1] and C[0..2] are determined, and so is the sum publicAmount + relayFee. root is determined when at least one input is non-empty. publicAsset is determined when that sum is non-zero, and the contract requires zero when it is not. The circuit does not derive extHash, gRef, or how the sum is split between publicAmount and relayFee: these are public inputs that the sender chooses, that lemma 10 binds to the proof, and that the contract checks. Every intermediate signal is determined by the inputs.
  9. No wrap-around. No sum, product or difference reaches the field modulus in constraints 8, 9, 12 and 13, in the sum publicAmount + relayFee of constraint 14, in the packed word, in the emptiness test or in the position. The other factor of constraint 14 may wrap when both amounts are zero, and that does no harm.
  10. Every public input counts. Each of the 11 occurs in a constraint that survives compilation, and their columns in the constraint system are linearly independent, so the key holds a separate point for each one and a proof made for one set of public inputs does not verify for another. One pair needs care. publicAmount and relayFee enter constraints 12 and 14 only as their sum. Their separate range checks in constraint 3 are what give them separate columns, and both checks stay. If a builder or a compiler setting removed them, the same proof would verify with value moved from the withdrawal to the relay fee or back, and the relayer named in a proof could take part of the recipient's withdrawal. The lemma is stated for the constraint system that goes into the ceremony, where the check is simple: the eleven public-input columns have rank eleven. Part B checks the same thing on the key.
  11. Honest spends are accepted. Stated with every hypothesis written out, and none of them about a signal of the circuit. Take any tree, any one or two notes in it that share one sk and one asset, any gRef, and any choice of outputs, claim, publicAmount and relayFee. Suppose that, as whole numbers: every value, accrued and claim is under 296; each input's index is at most gRef, and gRef is under 290; weight × (value₀ × (gRef − index₀) + value₁ × (gRef − index₁)) is under 2160; value and reward balance as constraints 12 and 13 say; and two real inputs have different positions. Then the witness generator produces an assignment that satisfies the constraint system. The witness generator is frozen by hash together with the statements, so that "the honest witness" cannot be chosen to suit a proof. Without this lemma a range check one bit too tight would lock notes for good while every other lemma still held. That the pool only ever reaches states in which honest spends meet these conditions is not a property of the circuit. It is in part D.

B. Checked on the verification key and the deployed verifier. These are facts about the ceremony's output and cannot be read off the circuit. The 12 input points of the key are pairwise different and none is the point at infinity. Every point is on its curve and the G2 points are in the right subgroup. Alpha, beta, gamma and delta are not the point at infinity, gamma and delta differ, and delta is not the generator. The key is recomputed from the final ceremony file by a second tool, and the chain of contributions is verified from the first file to the last. On the deployed verifier, changing any one public input of a valid proof makes it fail, and so does raising publicAmount by any amount while lowering relayFee by the same.

C. Assumed, named, and not machine-checked. That Groth16 proofs can only be made by someone who knows a satisfying assignment, which rests on the ceremony having had at least one honest contributor. That a proof seen for one set of public inputs does not help anyone who lacks the witness to make a proof for another. Groth16 has that property when the public-input columns are independent, which lemma 10 checks, and every claim that a relayer cannot alter a spend it was handed rests on it and not on the first assumption. That nobody can find two inputs with the same Poseidon hash, or an input for a given hash. Each appears in the formal statements as a written hypothesis and never as a hidden axiom.

D. Properties of the Pool contract, covered by tests, fuzzing and the audits. No nullifier is accepted twice, the two in one call are different, and no public input at or above p is accepted. g stays under its bound for every sequence of calls. For each asset the values of all unspent notes add up to at most shielded. And in every state the pool can reach, every spend an honest wallet can form meets the conditions of lemma 11. The one condition that needs an argument is the bound of 2160. It follows from four facts: note values add up to at most shielded; an input's index is at most g; reward earned in total is at most released, which is at most the users' 45%; and the supply times 1018 is under 296. The argument is written out in the formal specification and the fuzzing job checks it.

The six contracts

None has an owner, a pause, an upgrade path or a setter for any parameter. Every state-changing function is guarded against re-entry. They call three Poseidon hash contracts that already exist on mainnet. A one-time deployer creates five of them and then has no function left, and the Distribution contract creates the token at launch. Each of the five works out the others' addresses from the Deployer's address. Each reads the token's address from Distribution.token() the first time it needs it and keeps it. Until the token exists, deposits, registrations, stakes and fee sweeps revert.

The fee ceiling. For ETH it is (4 × block.basefee + 1 gwei) × 2,000,000 param. That is 0.002 ETH plus about seven times what a spend costs at the base fee. The fixed part is there so that a relayer can still be paid a tip when the base fee is close to nothing, and at such times it is most of the ceiling: at a base fee of 0.05 gwei the ceiling is about 0.0024 ETH, some forty times the cost of the gas. For another asset the ceiling is eight times the ETH figure param, converted at a rate frozen in the build and rounded down: the number of base units one ETH bought on the freeze day, and for the pool's token the rate at the starting valuation, about 16.8 million tokens per ETH at a supply of one billion. The factor of eight is room for prices to move. The 0.2% exit fee is charged on the relay fee like on everything else that leaves.

What the ceiling does and does not do. It is a limit per spend, and there is no limit per day. A holder can name an address of their own as the relayer and take out up to the ceiling in each spend, in any amount, paying the 0.2% and the gas each time: about $5 a spend in ETH and about $40 in another asset at today's base fee. So standard sizes are what the wallet uses and what every withdrawal has, and the relay fee is a second, small way out beside them. Whoever uses it shows exact amounts and weakens their own privacy and nobody else's. It is also how a remainder below the smallest size can leave. The ceiling does not protect a user from a relayer that charges too much, which is the wallet's job. And relaying an asset other than ETH depends on the frozen rate: if the asset's price in ETH falls by a factor of about fifty while gas is dear, or of a few hundred while gas is cheap, no relayer can be paid enough in that asset, and its holders send their spends themselves from a public address.

Invariants.

  • For each capped asset, the pool's balance is at least pending + shielded + fees, plus owed in total for ETH. For the reward token it is at least the users' 45% plus pending + fees + tokenIn − tokenOut.
  • A spend can never take out more of a capped asset than shielded, and never more of the reward token than tokenIn + released − tokenOut.
  • For each capped asset, the values of all unspent notes add up to at most shielded.
  • shielded[asset] never exceeds the asset's cap at the time it last grew. Caps never fall.
  • Only its depositor can activate a deposit. A refund goes to the depositor whoever calls it. Each deposit ends in exactly one of the two.
  • A nullifier is marked once and never cleared. Zero is never marked, and neither is a number at or above p. The tree only grows, by one leaf per activation or spend.

  • Nothing leaves through the relay fee beyond the ceiling in force in that block.
  • After the deposit window closes no deposit is created. Activation, refund, roll, spend and claimOwed have no end date, and no state the pool can reach makes roll revert.
  • No function sends an asset anywhere except: a refund to its depositor, a withdrawal to the recipient and a relay fee to the relayer named in a verified proof, a credit to the address it was recorded for, and fees to the distributor.
  • No proof is accepted before the Verifier exists: a call to an address without code counts as a failed verification.

2. Verifier

The Groth16 verifier for Spend, generated from the ceremony's output. One view function, verify(proof, inputs[11]). No storage. Its invariant is that it accepts exactly the proofs the verification key accepts, which is tested against the reference verifier with valid, altered and malformed proofs. A public input at or above the field modulus makes verify return false, whatever a reference verifier does with such an input. The checks on the key itself are part B of what must be shown.

3. ScreenerRegistry

Decides which deposits may be activated. Holds screener stakes, the screeners' share of fees and the screeners' 15% of the token. It cannot reach anything in the pool. If it fails, deposits stop and nothing already shielded is affected. Before GENESIS every function reverts.

An epoch is one day from GENESIS, in three windows. Commit runs from hour 0 up to hour 12, reveal from hour 12 up to hour 20, and apply from hour 20 to the end. Each window includes its first second and not its last. A batch can be applied only in the apply window of its own epoch, when every reveal is in. A batch that is accepted and not applied by the end of its day is dropped and pays nobody, and the next day's batch covers the same deposits. So no batch depends on another, and everything about an epoch is final when it ends. No function finishes an epoch afterwards.

One record per epoch. epochs[e] is written during epoch e only, and is never changed or reused afterwards. It holds the snapshot, the hash with the most reveals and its count, the accepted hash if there is one, how many revealed it, whether it was applied, and the pay per head in each asset. An epoch in which nobody called the registry has no record: no majority hash, no strikes and no pay.

The roll. Every state-changing function except flag and signalCriteria begins with it. If this is the first call of the current epoch e: activeCount rises by joining, joining becomes zero, epochs[e].snap is set to activeCount, and rolledEpoch becomes e. It does nothing else, however many epochs have passed since the last call. The snapshot does not move during the epoch, whoever leaves or is removed.

Ending a record. Three things, always together. If activeFrom is not zero and at most the current epoch, activeCount falls by one, and if it is the next epoch, joining falls by one. The stake moves to unbonding[holder][current epoch + 14]. The record is deleted. The current epoch's snapshot is not touched, so a group that leaves during the day does not lower the threshold for those who stay. A record cannot be ended in an epoch in which it has revealed, because its pay for that epoch is not fixed until apply.

Settling. Done at the start of commit, requestExit and evict, and by settle. It looks at past epochs only, never the current one, in four steps.

  1. Pay. If the record's last reveal was in a past epoch that is not yet settled, that epoch's batch was applied, and the revealed hash is its accepted hash, the epoch's pay per head in each asset moves from reserved to owed[holder].
  2. Silence. Take the later of the record's last reveal epoch and the epoch before activeFrom. If seven or more whole epochs have passed since then, the record is ended and the seat is barred.
  3. Strikes. Otherwise, for each past epoch not yet settled in which the record was active, of which there can be at most seven: if the epoch has a majority hash and the record did not reveal it, the seat gains three strikes. If the record did reveal it and the epoch has an accepted hash, the seat loses one strike, down to zero.
  4. Removal. settledThrough becomes the epoch before the current one. If the seat now has 21 strikes or more, the record is ended and the seat is barred.

Barring sets barredUntil to the current epoch plus 90 and the seat's strikes to zero. An epoch's majority hash is its lead hash when more than half of its snapshot revealed it. An accepted hash is always the majority hash. A commit settles, and a reveal needs a commit, so a screener that takes part is settled every day and a gap of more than seven epochs is always silence. That is what keeps every settlement to seven epochs at most.

What the strikes bound. A group that is more than a third and less than half can stop acceptance on any day by revealing nothing or by revealing something else. The honest majority's hash is still the majority hash that day, so each member that withheld gains three strikes. A member earns a strike back only on a day when a batch is accepted, so resting on a day the group blocks earns nothing. Seven misses reach 21. A group of just over a third can therefore block for a week at a stretch. A group of just under half can block for up to ten days, because its members take turns at withholding. After that the group has been removed or has to let batches through, and over time it can block at most about one day in three: a quarter of days for a bare third, 30% for 14 of 30, 33% for 50 of 101. The figures are from reports/sim/strike_rule.py. What the rule cannot prevent is that share of lost days, for ever. An even split is a deadlock: neither half is a majority, nobody gains a strike, and no batch is accepted until a seat changes sides or leaves. And a group that holds a majority for a week can remove everyone else.

Pay. An epoch pays when its batch was both accepted and applied, and the amounts are fixed inside apply. For each asset, the pot is divided by the number of screeners who revealed the accepted hash. The result is written to the epoch's record as the pay per head, that amount times the number of revealers moves from pot to reserved, and the remainder stays in the pot. For the token, one share of the 15% is added to the pot first, while fewer than 730 shares have been paid. Each revealer is credited at its next settlement, to the address of the holder who registered the seat. There is no deadline for that. A day without an applied batch pays nothing and loses nothing: its fees stay in the pot for the next applied batch, and its token share is paid on a later day. Blocking a day therefore delays every screener's pay, the blockers' included, and removes none of it. The penalty for blocking is the strikes.

After deposits close. Commits are open until 30 epochs param after the deposit window closes, and without limit while START is not yet set. Whatever is left of the 15% then stays locked for good, and so does whatever reached the pot after the last applied batch, which is at most a few days of the screeners' fee share.

Invariants.

  • Every receipt is split 40, 40 and 20, with any rounding remainder going to the basket stream.
  • For each asset, the balance is at least accounted: the unpaid staker stream plus everything owed plus the basket and its stream, plus the staked tokens for the token and buybackImd for IMD.
  • A staker stream pays out everything put into it, to within one base unit per update, for any asset and any amount staked.
  • Staked tokens return only to the staker who staked them.
  • buybackImd falls only through a swap in the locked pool, and no function sends it to an address. IMD owed to stakers and screeners is accounted apart from it.
  • A buyback step never exceeds half of the pool's fee rate times the locked position's virtual IMD reserve at the larger of the current and the starting price, and at most one step runs per 12 hours.
  • A buyer receives only assets it named, and only their released basket balance.

6. Token

A plain ERC-20 with 18 decimals, created by the Distribution contract at launch. The whole supply param is created for its creator in the constructor. There is no owner, no mint, no burn function, no fee on transfer and no blocklist. Its invariants are that total supply never changes and that no address has any power another does not. The name and symbol are constants of the build, and the canary's are different from the final ones.

Rules for the wallet and the relayer

These are not enforced by any contract. A wallet or relayer that ignores them loses privacy or gas, so they are part of the specification of both.

  • One proving policy. The wallet proves against the current checkpoint, rootCur and gCur. If the current hourly window has no checkpoint yet, it calls roll first, or has the relayer do it. A spend that lands after the next roll shows the earlier checkpoint, which tells observers the proof was made before that roll and nothing more.
  • Fee asset. The relayer is paid in the asset being spent. Before the first relayed spend of an asset, the wallet tells the user that relaying shows which asset moved. It offers the alternative of sending the spend from a public address, which shows that address and no asset.
  • Quotes. A relayer's quote names an amount of the asset being spent and an expiry, and the wallet puts that expiry into the proof as the deadline, so a relayer cannot hold a spend and land it later. The wallet refuses a quote above three times the gas cost of the spend at the current base fee and tip, whatever the contract's ceiling allows, and a quote above the ceiling less a margin for a falling base fee. The quote allows for the checkpoint write that the first transaction of an hour pays for.
  • One relayer at a time. The wallet does not give the same inputs to a second relayer until the first spend's deadline has passed. Two transactions with the same nullifiers cost the second relayer its gas.
  • Accrued reward stays with the sender. In a payment, output 0 carries no accrued reward. All of it goes to the change output, which may have a value of zero, or is claimed. Otherwise the recipient would learn how much the sender had earned.
  • Before relaying, a relayer simulates the spend. A payment in ETH that the recipient refuses does not make the spend fail: the amount waits in owed.
  • Edges. The wallet deposits in standard sizes by default, spreads exits over time, warns before several exits go to one address, and warns before reward tokens are withdrawn to an address linked to other exits, because a withdrawal of reward tokens shows how much value was held for how long. Before a deposit of USDT or USDC it reads whether the token is paused and whether the pool's address is frozen, and refuses if either is true, because USDT would accept the deposit and the pool could not return it.

Deployment and the canary

  1. Build. One reproducible build produces the creation code of the contracts, the circuit and a parameter file. The ceremony runs on that circuit, and the verifier's code is generated from its output. The final build fixes GENESIS. It differs from the build that was audited and run as the canary in a short list of named constants and nothing else, and a job checks that by comparing bytecode.
  2. Deployer. A small contract is created through the deterministic deployment proxy at 0x4e59…956C. Its constructor fixes five creation-code hashes in order. Its only function, deploy(i, code), creates contract i with CREATE if code matches hash i and every earlier one exists, and reverts if creation fails. No constructor takes an argument and none calls another contract. Each contract takes the Deployer to be whoever created it and works out the other four addresses from that and the fixed order, so the hashes do not depend on themselves. Anyone can send these transactions, and nobody can deploy anything else through it.
  3. One contract per transaction: the Deployer, then Verifier, ScreenerRegistry, FeeDistributor, Pool and Distribution. Under Glamsterdam pricing they do not fit in one transaction or one block.
  4. Launch. From GENESIS, anyone calls Distribution.launch(), which creates the token and the public pool in one transaction. Whoever sends it can also be the first buyer in the same transaction, and nothing in a permissionless launch prevents that. Screeners then buy their stake, register and wait 30 days. The first batch can be applied about 33 days after launch, and that moment is START.
  5. Publication. The build page lists the salt and the five addresses that exist before launch. The token's address is fixed only by the launch transaction and is read from Distribution.token(). The wallet module has the Pool's address built in and shows it. This matters because every constant but GENESIS is public long before launch: anyone can assemble a build that is identical apart from that time, deploy it early and launch it, and nothing on-chain says which deployment is the project's. The address is the only difference a user can check.

The canary

The canary is the whole system from the same source, and it comes after the ceremony: none of its changed constants is in the circuit, so it runs on the final circuit and the final verification key. Someone who finds a flaw in the circuit can therefore rehearse it on the canary before using it on the pool. These constants differ and nothing else does: the salt, GENESIS, the token's name and symbol, the swarm list's Merkle root (a test list), a cap step of 10 days instead of 90, an emission period of 81 days and a deposit window of 85, starting caps of 0.5 ETH, 1,000 USDT, 1,000 USDC and 50 IMD, and a screener wait of 3 days instead of 30. Its nodes also start from a different criteria file, which is a value in the parameter file and in no contract: the canary's file refuses one published test address, so that a refusal can be seen. In the 90 days after its START the canary passes every cap step, the end of emissions and the close of deposits. It has its own token and its own pool against IMD, which anyone could buy from, so the lawyer's review comes before the canary.

It needs seven registered seats before anything can be activated. The project cannot supply or arrange them and can only wait, and one holder could supply all seven. Because the schedules count from START, the wait costs nothing.

The project takes part as a user and nothing else. It deposits, spends and withdraws from its own funds, at least 10 ETH in value, which is enough to fill every cap through the third step. It runs no screener and no relayer. Lines it can complete by itself, each with the amount that must be paid checked to the base unit:

  • Every external function of the Pool, the FeeDistributor, the Distribution contract and the Token.
  • Spends of every kind: two real inputs; one real and one empty; with a relay fee paid in each of the five assets; with a relay fee from a wallet that holds a single note; sent by the owner with no relay fee; with a reward claim; against the previous checkpoint; of token notes; of a freshly activated note; and a withdrawal of every asset at its smallest and largest reachable size.
  • A deposit refunded by its depositor. An activation that fails at a full cap and succeeds after a withdrawal.
  • A payment in ETH to a contract that refuses it, credited and later claimed. A refund of ETH to a contract whose receiving code needs more than 50,000 gas, credited and claimed in the same way.
  • A spent proof sent again with the field modulus added to one nullifier, rejected. A spend sent after its deadline, rejected.
  • A basket sale, a sale that leaves one asset out, buyback steps down to an empty balance, and a staker's claim in USDT.
  • A wallet restored from its seed alone that recovers every note, including a payment it received from a second wallet, and then spends that payment.
  • Spends and refunds after deposits have closed and emissions have ended.

The project runs no relayer, so in its own lines it names an address of its own as the relayer. That exercises the payment and not a third party's relaying. Spends carried by seat broadcasters are recorded when they happen.

Lines that depend on what screeners do. They are recorded when they happen on mainnet, and each is also run on a fork so that the result does not wait on anyone's behaviour: a batch applied every day; a flag posted by a seat that is still waiting, counted in the first batch; the test address's deposit refused, and sent back by a third party after 21 days; a day with no accepted batch, and a day with no call to the registry at all, each followed by a normal day; a screener that exits before it becomes active; a screener removed for silence, one for strikes and one whose seat was sold; and the pay each screener receives.

Paths that cannot be reached on mainnet in the time, run on a fork of mainnet state with the results published: a launch with pools already created for its first candidates; launch under the gas rules in force; a batch in which more than 96 words set a bit; a stablecoin issuer freezing the pool, and freezing a depositor; the end of the two-year vesting; the 30-day screener wait and the 90-day bar; and the end of commits after deposits close.

What the canary shows: the deployment procedure works, the contracts and the circuit agree on mainnet for the spends in the list, the amounts paid are the amounts specified, and nobody who looked took a few thousand dollars. What it does not show: that the circuit is sound, how the registry behaves with screeners who work against it, or what happens at real volumes. The proofs are for the first, and the caps limit the cost if the proofs are wrong.

The node and the wallet

One node, run by a seat holder, has three duties: relay, mirror and screen. One wallet module does everything else on the user's device. Before the pool launches, the same node and wallet serve Railgun and Privacy Pools, which is how the project earns a place in wallets. None of this is a contract.

Identity without a registry

Admission is derived, never granted, and it is checked by whoever relies on it. A seat is the identity.md NFT at 0x0000ec93…ec1d. Its source is verified, it is not a proxy, and its owner() is the CREATE2 deployment proxy it was created through, so its owner-only functions have no key behind them. The holder signs one delegation from the wallet that holds the seat. The node then works with a separate operational key, so the wallet holding the NFT never sits on a server.

// EIP-712 typed data. Domain { name: "Murmuration", version: "2", chainId: 1 }; no verifyingContract.
SeatDelegation {
  uint256 seatId;          // identity.md tokenId
  address opKey;           // secp256k1 key the node signs with. Not the holder key, not IMD's device key
  string  railgunAddress;  // 0zk address that signs fee messages and receives fee notes ("" if unused)
  address ppFeeRecipient;  // Privacy Pools relay fee recipient (0x0 if unused)
  uint8   duties;          // bit 0 relay, bit 1 mirror, bit 2 attest
  uint64  issuedAt;        // the highest issuedAt per seat wins
  uint64  notAfter;        // at most issuedAt + 90 days
}

Verification, by any wallet or relying party

  1. Read ownerOf(seatId) at a recent finalized block, through a light client or two independent RPC endpoints.
  2. Check the signature against that owner. Use ecrecover when the owner has no code and an ERC-1271 isValidSignature call when it has. The second path covers Safes and EIP-7702 accounts, and stays correct if EIP-8151 changes ecrecover for accounts with code.
  3. Check now ≤ notAfter ≤ issuedAt + 90 days. A transferred seat voids its delegation automatically, because the owner no longer matches.
  4. Group seats by holder and count each holder once. Optionally require that the holder has held the seat for 30 days, read from Transfer logs.

Publication

Delegations need a public bulletin board, not a trusted registry. The default is an attestation on the existing Ethereum Attestation Service contract (0xA1207F3B…e587, about 231,000 gas each) that carries the signed struct. Clients verify the inner signature themselves, so EAS is relied on for availability and nothing else. The same struct can travel over any other channel.

What a seat is worth as an identity

  • Cost per identity: the floor, 0.70 ETH on 10 November 2026 (about $1,490), down from 0.796 on 3 November. A seat can be resold, so the real cost of an attack is capital tied up for its duration plus price impact. Seven-day volume on 8 November was about $79,000, fewer than 50 seats.
  • Distinct holders are a convenience, not a defence. A holder can move seats to fresh wallets for the price of a transfer. The number that matters is seats multiplied by the floor. The 30-day holding filter raises the time cost and nothing more.
  • Not in the trust path: IMD's ERC-8004 adapter (a proxy upgradeable by a three-of-four Safe), the ERC-8004 registries (proxies owned by one externally owned account), the Ed25519 device key in IMD's config file, and IMD's API.

Broadcaster mesh protocol

What a node runs, unchanged

  • Railgun broadcaster 8.2.0 from gitlab.com/railgun-protocol. The GitHub copy stops at 8.0.0 and points at the wrong Waku cluster.
  • nwaku 0.37.1 on Railgun's cluster 5, shard 1, with Railgun's content topics (/railgun/v2/0-1-fees/json and the matching transact topics). That cluster does not use RLN.
  • A PPOI node URL and an RPC endpoint of the operator's own. The broadcaster reads the URL from POI_NODE_URL and ships with it empty. Railgun's documentation publishes a community node at ppoi.fdi.network that any operator can use. The broadcaster refuses any transaction without a valid proof of innocence for the required list.
  • The open-source Privacy Pools relayer, if the operator also relays there.

What Murmuration adds, in the wallet

Railgun's client library already takes a list of trusted fee signers and drops quotes outside a band around their average. Railway fills that list from its own remote configuration. The policy below fills it from seat delegations and changes two rules.

signers  = feeMessages.filter(m => validDelegation(m.railgunAddress))   // steps 1 to 4 above
byHolder = oneSignerPerHolder(signers)                                 // a holder counts once
band     = byHolder.filter(s => 0.90*median <= s.fee <= 1.30*median)   // Railgun's band, around the median
pick     = uniformRandom(band)                                         // fresh local randomness per attempt
prove(tx, feeNoteTo = pick.railgunAddress); send(pick)
on refusal or timeout:
    remember(pick, 24h)                    // local memory only; nothing is reported anywhere
    pick again and prove again             // the fee note names one broadcaster, so a retry is a new proof
  • Median, not mean. A few hostile seats cannot drag a median the way they drag an average.
  • Random, not cheapest. Railgun's default takes the lowest fee. With open admission that hands every transaction to whoever quotes one percent under the rest. One seat, 0.70 ETH, would see all traffic first.
  • No seed. The draw uses fresh randomness every time, so a retry does not land on the broadcaster that just refused.

What a broadcaster can and cannot do

  • It cannot redirect funds or take a larger fee. Its fee is a commitment proven inside the transaction. Railgun's bound parameters do not name a submitter, so a copier who lands the transaction first only wastes the broadcaster's gas.
  • It sees the full transaction before inclusion: the unshield recipient, the calldata of a RelayAdapt swap, the PPOI proofs. It can refuse, delay, or trade ahead of a swap. Wallets should set tight minimum-output limits on relayed swaps.
  • An adversary with k seats among N honest broadcasters sees about k/(N+k) of relayed traffic, for k times 0.70 ETH of resalable capital. With ten seats among thirty honest ones that is a quarter of traffic for about 7 ETH.
  • It does not learn the sender's IP if the wallet reaches Waku through Tor. Railway Wallet ships no Tor or mixnet today, and the mesh cannot fix that from the operator side.

Public snapshots

  • Deterministic. Events are serialized in canonical order with fixed encoding, so identical chain data gives an identical content identifier. Two nodes that agree need no signature. Two that disagree are both visible.
  • Verified by the wallet. The wallet rebuilds the commitment tree from the chunks and compares the root with the pool's on-chain root at toBlock.
  • Nothing to hide. Every user fetches the same chunks. A mirror or gateway learns that someone uses the pool, and no more. Fetch over Tor to hide that too.
  • Covers history expiry. Pools' logs stay available even when full nodes stop serving old ones.
  • Integration. Railgun's engine 9.9.1 takes its quick-sync source as two arguments of initForWallet: quickSyncEvents and quickSyncRailgunTransactionsV2. Wallet SDK 11.3.0 passes its own subgraph functions with no option, so an application reassigns the two properties on the engine after start, or patches that one call. The engine checks commitments against on-chain roots, so a snapshot keeps event-batch boundaries. It does not check nullifier or unshield events, so the wallet module does. This was read in the code and not run.

Remaining interactive reads (gas price, a public balance before shielding) go through anon-rpc and Helios in the wallet. Helios verifies answers and does not hide the question, which is why transport privacy comes from the anon-rpc worker.

Per-transaction disclosure receipt

// Made by the wallet, for one transaction and one recipient. Nothing is stored anywhere.
DisclosureReceipt {
  uint256 chainId;  address pool;  bytes32 txHash;
  NoteOpening[] notes;     // per note: asset, value, owner key, randomness, leaf index, Merkle path
  bytes32 depositLabel;    // Privacy Pools only: links the withdrawal to its deposit

  bytes   signature;       // by the address that received the unshield, or by the spending key
}

The recipient recomputes each commitment from its opening, checks it sits in the pool's tree, and checks the nullifier on-chain. There is precedent: Tornado Cash's compliance tool produces a per-note report, and Railgun already has user-held viewing keys. A receipt is narrower than a viewing key because it covers one transaction. It can be forwarded like any document, and it cannot be produced without the user. For the Murmuration pool a receipt opens commitments and does not let its reader check nullifiers: that would need the wallet's nullifier key, which is the same for every note the wallet has.

The node runs beside the IMD worker, not through it. IMD's tool declaration is a local MCP server for media jobs that only IMD's router reads, so it cannot advertise a role.

  • Separate keys. IMD's worker runs model-driven tasks on public briefs and its own README says to keep nothing valuable on that machine. The node never reads IMD's device key, and the seat-holding wallet signs one delegation offline.

What no design choice removes

  • The edges are public. Every deposit and every withdrawal shows an address, an amount and a time, to everyone. Fixed exit sizes hide the amount link. They do not hide that a withdrawal happened.
  • Timing in a small crowd. If few people use the pool, a deposit followed soon by a withdrawal can be guessed without any operator's help. The wallet's delay and its pool-health warnings help, and only a larger crowd fixes it.
  • The user's own habits. Withdrawing to an address already tied to the deposit address undoes everything. The wallet warns before it happens.

Where this revision disagrees with a decision

The owner's decisions are built as decided. Three notes are recorded here because the evidence supports them, and none is acted on.

  • Stakers are paid in the asset the fee was taken in, as decided. Selling the whole fee for IMD in the one auction and paying stakers and screeners in IMD would remove most of the accounting in the distributor and the registry, and would tie the project more closely to IMD. It would also expose all fee income to IMD's price. It is an option, not a recommendation.
  • The canary places a public token pool. A canary that exercises the token contracts has to launch a token. The decision to rely on a canary therefore brings the lawyer's review forward to before the canary.
  • The stake of 0.03%. The public pool holds a quarter of supply, which is 833 stakes of this size, and each stake bought raises the price of the next. Until rewards and vesting put other tokens into circulation, the registry cannot grow past a few hundred screeners, and whoever buys early, the sender of the launch transaction first of all, sets the price of entry for everyone after. A smaller stake would avoid both. It is built as decided.
  • DAI was left out. It is the least used of the three stablecoins and the only one no issuer can freeze. With USDT and USDC only, every dollar in the pool can be frozen by its issuer. That is the owner's choice and keeps the exposure at four capped assets.

Legal position

Legal gate: requirements every component must pass

The record, stated carefully. The Fifth Circuit's Van Loon ruling protects only immutable contracts and is a sanctions ruling, not a criminal one. Roman Storm was convicted on one count, conspiracy to operate an unlicensed money transmitting business, under a theory that turns on knowledge. The verdict was general, the Rule 29 motion is still pending and a retrial on the hung counts is set for 26 April 2027. Prosecutors' account of his conduct included paying to host the website, maintaining the relayer network and profiting from it. The Dutch court in Pertsev's case held that Tornado Cash itself did the concealing. The DOJ's 2025 statements are discretionary. The statutory safe harbour failed cloture 49 to 50 on 15 September 2026 and lapses with this Congress on 3 January 2027.

  • The pool, the token and the fee distributor are immutable: no admin, pause, upgrade, fee switch or mint function.
  • The project team writes software and operates nothing: no node, relayer, mirror, screener or hosted frontend. The software ships as a node package and a wallet module.
  • The team does not recruit, pay or coordinate operators. Roadmap gates are observations read from the chain.
  • A fixed fee of 0.2% in and 0.2% out and a token exist. No fee goes to a team address. The founder holds 5% of the token, vested in code over two years, at one public address.
  • 25% of the token is offered to the public through a locked pool paired with IMD. No external audit firm reviews the code. Assurance comes from IMD audit panels, machine-checked proofs, a canary and very low caps.
  • Screening is real, published and executed by independent screeners. The pool contract enforces it when a deposit is activated, and seat broadcasters enforce PPOI on Railgun.
  • No running part is brokered through IMD's control plane. IMD jobs are bought only to build and review open-source code. Admission is derived from on-chain seat ownership.
  • Disclosure is user-held, opt-in and scoped to one transaction. No key share exists anywhere outside the user's wallet.
  • No support channel through which the team learns about specific flows.

Two standing rules are reversed by the owner's decision of 10 November 2026: "no developer fee in any token" and "no project token". The review advised against both, and against combining a founder allocation with pseudonymity. Immutability decides whether anyone controls the code. It does not decide who profits from it, and profit is what enforcement has looked at. The reasoning and the mitigations are in Token and fee. Two more departures from the review followed: a public token pool, and no external audit.

Operators carry the exposure the developers avoid. No guidance or ruling found says whether a fee-earning relayer is a money transmitter. FinCEN's 2019 guidance separates anonymizing software providers, who are not, from anonymizing services providers, who are, and never mentions relayers. At the Rule 29 hearing on 9 April 2026 the government called relayers "part of the cohort operating Tornado Cash... for a profit". Each operator needs advice under their own law.

Two rules were tested against new evidence and kept. Immutability has a cost, shown when the ownerless Aztec Connect contracts were drained with no way to stop it, and the plan answers that with one small circuit, formal verification, per-asset limits, immutable caps and a fixed deposit window. Against that cost, on 7 October 2026 the EU's AML authority told the Commission that "effective control" over a DeFi arrangement may arise from "the ability to pause or modify smart contracts". The rule stays.

This page is not legal advice. A lawyer reviews the fee, the token, the public pool and the founder allocation before launch. Because the canary places a token pool of its own, that review now comes before the canary.

What the node and wallet must not assume

  • That a pool's transaction costs the same gas after a fork as before it.
  • That refunds reduce the cost of a transaction.
  • That ecrecover returns an address for every signer.
  • That full nodes serve logs from years ago.
  • That a quote made in one slot is included in the next.
  • For the pool: that any contract above 24 KiB can be deployed, or that any single transaction can carry more than 16,777,216 execution gas.

Found along the way

  • IMD's supply is fixed, and one key administers its bridge. IMD has no mint function, and about 7.08 million tokens exist across Ethereum, Base and Robinhood Chain, 4.22 million of them on Ethereum on 11 November. The Ethereum token is a LayerZero bridged token: it is credited when a registered bridge peer reports a transfer, and its owner, a single key, can change the registered peers. Nothing suggests that key has been misused. It is a trust assumption that renouncing ownership would remove. The token has no transfer tax, blocklist or pause. Its main market is an unlocked Uniswap v4 pool of about $1.14M. The public pool and the buyback are both priced in IMD.
  • Seats are concentrated. 2,000 seats sit at 791 addresses. The largest holds 77, the top five 243, the top ten 382, and 38 addresses hold ten or more. One address could register more screeners than a launch set of 15 independent ones needs to be outvoted two to one.
  • Anyone can pre-create the token's Uniswap pool. Uniswap v3's factory does not check that a token exists, and a pool's first price is set by whoever calls first. Checked in the v3 core source. Revision 4.2 creates the token inside the launch transaction for this reason, and revision 4.3 adds the loop over candidate addresses, because an address that depends only on the block and the caller can be read by anyone inside that block.
  • The token's place in its pool is a matter of address order. Uniswap orders a pool's two tokens by address, and IMD's begins 0xd34a, so a random address sorts below it about 82.5% of the time. The launch takes only such an address.
  • The sandwich bound. A buyback step no larger than the pool's fee rate times its IMD reserves cannot be sandwiched at a profit. Checked numerically in reports/sim/buyback_sandwich_bound.py for a single position. The build measures the reserve from the locked position's own liquidity.
  • Setup mistakes have been exploited. In February 2026 two live Groth16 verifiers were drained because a setup step was skipped. The build plan checks the verification key explicitly.
  • FinCEN withdrew its 2020 recordkeeping and travel-rule proposal on 6 November, the third digital-asset proposal withdrawn in five weeks. It concerns financial institutions, not pools.

Open questions
