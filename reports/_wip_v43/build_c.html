When screeners reveal and apply on time, a deposit can be activated between a day and a half and three days after it is made. It takes longer when they do not, at a full cap, under a hold, and in the first 33 days after launch. The batch that covers a deposit uses data up to the start of the second epoch after it, so screeners always have at least one full day, and up to two, in which a flag or a list update still counts. One case has no way out: a depositor that cannot receive its own asset back, such as an address a stablecoin issuer has frozen. That deposit stays in the pool, unactivated, for good.

Empty inputs are never in the tree. A note whose ciphertext was posted wrongly is Created for ever and nobody can spend it. Reward stops growing for everyone when emissions end, and a note keeps what it has accrued. Reward on a small balance may never be worth the gas of withdrawing it.

Rules for the wallet and the relayer

These are not enforced by any contract. A wallet or relayer that ignores them loses privacy or gas, so they are part of the specification of both.

  • One proving policy. The wallet always proves against the checkpoint root and gCur. If the current hourly window has no checkpoint yet, it calls roll first, or has the relayer do it. It waits for the next roll before spending a note newer than the checkpoint. Mixing recent roots and checkpoint roots would tell observers which spends use notes less than an hour old.
  • Fee asset. The wallet pays the relayer in ETH whenever it holds an ETH note, and keeps a small one for the purpose. Otherwise it pays in the asset it is spending and tells the user that this shows the asset.
  • Quotes. A relayer's quote names an asset, an amount and an expiry. The wallet refuses a quote above the fee ceiling less a margin for a falling base fee. The quote allows for the checkpoint write that the first transaction of an hour pays for.
  • One relayer at a time. The wallet does not give the same inputs to a second relayer until the first quote has expired. Two transactions with the same nullifiers cost the second relayer its gas.
  • Before relaying, a relayer simulates the spend. A payment in ETH that the recipient refuses does not make the spend fail: the amount waits in owed.
  • Edges. The wallet deposits in standard sizes by default, spreads exits over time, warns before several exits go to one address, and warns before reward tokens are withdrawn to an address linked to other exits, because a withdrawal of reward tokens shows how much value was held for how long.

  • Version 4 overstated the cost of post-quantum encryption. It priced each note at about 70,000 gas "at the calldata floor". A spend pays the ordinary rate and publishes one recipient ciphertext, so the whole encryption overhead is about 31,500 gas, under 3% of a spend, today and under Glamsterdam.
  • The tree's first writes. The first 64 inserts each fill a new slot of the root window, and each tree level is filled once. Those early activations and spends cost one or two new slots more than the table says.
  • The hash contracts. The pool calls the three poseidon-solidity contracts that already exist on mainnet, pinned by code hash, so it deploys none. They are 23,478, 14,189 and 19,260 bytes. Their README says they are unaudited, so the build checks them against the circuit with shared test vectors and the audit reads the generator.
  • Size limit. The build fails if any contract's deployed code exceeds 24,576 bytes. Estimates: Pool 18 to 22 KiB, ScreenerRegistry 15 to 19, FeeDistributor 11 to 14, Distribution 9 to 12 including the token's creation code, Verifier about 4, Token under 3. The Pool is the one to watch.
  • Transaction limit. The build fails if any call can exceed 16,777,216 execution gas with the worst state that others can create. The heaviest calls are a relayed spend, apply with a full 96-word batch (about 2.1M today and 10.6M under Glamsterdam, of which 1.2M is execution gas), and launch, whose execution gas is a few million whatever anyone else does, because it crosses no ticks and its loop costs about 5,000 gas per candidate.
  • No refunds are relied on. Clearing a deposit record earns a refund under both rule sets. No limit or estimate in the code depends on it.
  • Deployment. About 65 KiB of new code: roughly 14M gas today and 100M under Glamsterdam across the transactions, under $50 either way at 0.2 gwei.

Deployment and the canary

  1. Build. One reproducible build produces the creation code of the contracts, the circuit and a parameter file. The ceremony runs on that circuit, and the verifier's code is generated from its output. The final build fixes GENESIS. It differs from the build that was audited and run as the canary in a short list of named constants and nothing else, and a job checks that by comparing bytecode.
  2. Deployer. A small contract is created through the deterministic deployment proxy at 0x4e59…956C. Its constructor fixes five creation-code hashes in order. Its only function, deploy(i, code), creates contract i with CREATE if code matches hash i and every earlier one exists, and reverts if creation fails. No constructor takes an argument and none calls another contract. Each contract takes the Deployer to be whoever created it and works out the other four addresses from that and the fixed order, so the hashes do not depend on themselves. Anyone can send these transactions, and nobody can deploy anything else through it.
  3. One contract per transaction: the Deployer, then Verifier, ScreenerRegistry, FeeDistributor, Pool and Distribution. Under Glamsterdam pricing they do not fit in one transaction or one block.
  4. Launch. From GENESIS, anyone calls Distribution.launch(), which creates the token and the public pool in one transaction. Whoever sends it can also be the first buyer in the same transaction, and nothing in a permissionless launch prevents that. Screeners then buy their stake, register and wait 30 days. The first batch can be applied about 33 days after launch, and that moment is START.
  5. Publication. The build page lists the salt and the six addresses. The wallet module has the Pool's address built in and shows it. This matters because every constant but GENESIS is public long before launch: anyone can assemble a build that is identical apart from that time, deploy it early and launch it, and nothing on-chain says which deployment is the project's. The address is the only difference a user can check.

The canary

The canary is the whole system from the same source. These constants differ and nothing else does: the salt, GENESIS, the token's name and symbol, the swarm list's Merkle root (a test list), a cap step of 10 days instead of 90, an emission period of 81 days and a deposit window of 85, starting caps of 0.5 ETH, 1,000 USDT, 1,000 USDC and 50 IMD, and a screener wait of 3 days instead of 30. Its nodes also start from a different criteria file, which is a value in the parameter file and in no contract: the canary's file refuses one published test address, so that a refusal can be seen. In the 90 days after its START the canary passes every cap step, the end of emissions and the close of deposits. It has its own token and its own pool against IMD, which anyone could buy from, so the lawyer's review comes before the canary.

It needs seven registered seats before anything can be activated. The project cannot supply or arrange them and can only wait, and one holder could supply all seven. Because the schedules count from START, the wait costs nothing.

The project takes part as a user and nothing else. It deposits, spends and withdraws from its own funds, at least 10 ETH in value, which is enough to fill every cap through the third step. It runs no screener and no relayer. Lines it can complete by itself, each with the amount that must be paid checked to the base unit:

  • Every external function of the Pool, the FeeDistributor, the Distribution contract and the Token.
  • Spends of every kind: two real inputs; one real and one empty; through a relayer paid in ETH from a fee note; through a relayer paid in the spend's own asset by a wallet that holds a single note; sent by the owner; with a reward claim; against the previous checkpoint; of token notes; of a freshly activated note; and a withdrawal of every asset at its smallest and largest reachable size.
  • A deposit refunded by its depositor. An activation that fails at a full cap and succeeds after a withdrawal.
  • A payment in ETH to a contract that refuses it, credited and later claimed.
  • A basket sale, a sale that leaves one asset out, buyback steps down to an empty balance, and a staker's claim in USDT.
  • A wallet restored from its seed alone that recovers every note.
  • Spends and refunds after deposits have closed and emissions have ended.

Lines that depend on what screeners do. They are recorded when they happen on mainnet, and each is also run on a fork so that the result does not wait on anyone's behaviour: a batch applied every day; the test address's deposit refused, and sent back by a third party after 21 days; a day with no accepted batch; a screener removed for silence, one for strikes and one whose seat was sold; and the pay each screener receives.

Paths that cannot be reached on mainnet in the time, run on a fork of mainnet state with the results published: a launch with pools already created for its first candidates; launch under the gas rules in force; a batch in which more than 96 words set a bit; a stablecoin issuer freezing the pool, and freezing a depositor; the end of the two-year vesting; the 30-day screener wait and the 90-day bar; and the end of commits after deposits close.

What the canary shows: the deployment procedure works, the contracts and the circuit agree on mainnet for the spends in the list, the amounts paid are the amounts specified, and nobody who looked took a few thousand dollars. What it does not show: that the circuit is sound, how the registry behaves with screeners who work against it, or what happens at real volumes. The proofs are for the first, and the caps limit the cost if the proofs are wrong.
