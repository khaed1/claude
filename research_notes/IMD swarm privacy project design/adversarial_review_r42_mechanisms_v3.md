### M-1. Launch can be front-run by anyone who can order a transaction ahead of it, not only by the block builder (high)

Text. `[#b-contracts]` Distribution: "launch() | Anyone, once, from GENESIS | Creates the Token with CREATE2 and a salt taken from the block's prevrandao and the caller, so its address does not exist and cannot be known before this block. ... If someone in the same block has already created it at a higher price, sells at most a small bounded number of tokens param with the starting price as the limit ... and reverts the whole call if the price is still above the start." `[#token]` The public pool: "The token's address now does not exist and cannot be computed before the launch block, so there is nothing to prepare against. A block builder can still interfere inside that one block, which delays a launch attempt and cannot prevent the next." `[#review2]`: "Only someone building that same block can still interfere, and that costs them each time and delays one attempt."

Mechanism. The salt is a function of two values that every transaction in the launch block can read or be told: `block.prevrandao` (an opcode) and the caller (visible in the pending transaction). So an attacker does not need to know anything before the block. He deploys, once, a helper contract with one function `jam(caller)` that (1) computes the salt from `block.prevrandao` and `caller`, (2) derives the CREATE2 address from the Distribution address and the token init-code hash, both public, (3) calls `UniswapV3Factory.createPool(token, IMD, 10000)`, (4) calls `initialize` at a price far above the start, and (5) mints IMD-only bids above the starting price worth a little more than the "small bounded number of tokens". Checked against the local Uniswap source: `createPool` (`UniswapV3Factory.sol` line 35) has no access control and takes any address, `initialize` (`UniswapV3Pool.sol` line 271) is open to anyone once, and `mint` takes any recipient.

The helper call works in whatever block it lands in. Getting it ahead of the launch in the same block is ordinary front-running from the public mempool: a higher priority fee, or a two-transaction bundle `[jam, launch]` sent to builders, which any searcher can submit using someone else's public transaction. The launch then sells its bounded amount into the attacker's bids, finds the price still above the start and "reverts the whole call". Because the whole call reverts, the attacker's bids are untouched and he withdraws them in a third transaction. His cost is gas only: one v3 pool creation (about 4.5M gas) plus initialize and mint, about 5M gas, which is roughly 0.005 ETH or $11 at 1 gwei and about $1 at 0.1 gwei. The launcher loses the gas of a reverted launch each time. The next attempt has a new prevrandao and a new address, and the same helper handles it with no change.

A second variant needs no bids. The attacker lays dust positions on many ticks between his price and the start. The bounded sale has to cross every one of them. The invariant "The gas launch can use is bounded by what one block's worth of interference can add, and stays under the transaction limit" is stated without a number. An attacker can spend most of a block (60M gas or more) initialising ticks at roughly 85k gas each, and the launch must cross each at roughly 15k to 40k gas inside a single transaction capped at 2^24 = 16.78M gas. At a 60M block the margin is thin, and it shrinks with every increase of the block gas limit while the transaction cap stays. The contract can never be adjusted.

A third variant shows it is not confined to one block: the proposer of slot N-1 knows its own RANDAO reveal, so it knows `prevrandao` of block N while building N-1. A proposer that also builds can leave the launch out of N-1, create the pool there for the address the launch will produce in N, and block it from an earlier block.

What the launcher can do: send through private order flow, so that only the builders that receive it can jam it; or call through a contract so that `msg.sender` is a fresh address. The second does not help against anyone who simulates the pending transaction. The page says neither.

Who is "the caller" when a contract calls: `msg.sender`, the contract. A caller can therefore choose the contract address and grind the token address (vanity, or which side of IMD it sorts on, see M-9). The salt gives the caller control and gives nobody else any uncertainty.

Damage. Launch is delayed for as long as someone pays about 5M gas per attempt and wins ordering. With no token there is "no stake, no screener and no activation" (`#review2`), so the whole system waits. The clock does not wait: caps, emissions, the auction and the deposit window all count from GENESIS (M-12).

Fix (keeps every fixed decision, removes code). Make interference impossible instead of bounded: inside `launch`, loop a counter n = 0, 1, 2, ... into the salt and take the first candidate address for which `factory.getPool(candidate, IMD, 10000) == address(0)`, then create the token and the pool in that same transaction. A blocker has to pay a full pool deployment (about 4.5M gas) for each candidate, and can fit at most about 13 in a 60M block, while each check costs the launch about 5k gas. A loop bound of 64 is out of reach of any block. The "bounded price reset", its parameter, the `fundBuyback` call from launch and the gas invariant can then be deleted, because the pool cannot exist when the position is placed. Also correct the two sentences that say only a block builder can interfere.

### M-2. The flag rule lets one third of screeners (three seats while nine or fewer are active) refuse any named depositor, so control of entry costs a third, not two thirds (high)

Text. `[#screening]` criteria file: `"flags": { "source": "ScreenerRegistry.Flag events before epochStart, from active screeners", "quorum": "max(3, one third of active screeners)", "expireAfterEpochs": 14 }` and `{ "deny": "flagged(depositor)" }`. `[#b-contracts]` Registry: "flag(seatId, data), signalCriteria(seatId, hash) | The operator of an active screener | Emit an event and store nothing." `[#screening]`: "If screeners are bought. Whoever holds two thirds can approve a deposit that is at least a day old, and can refuse everyone else's deposits. Whoever holds a majority for a week can remove the rest and then do the same." and "control of the registry is also control of who earns the users' 45%." `[#b-contracts]` Pool: "refund(id, record) | ... Anyone, while the registry marks the deposit refused".

Mechanism. Refusing is not symmetric with approving. A bloc with one third of the active screeners posts a `Flag` event against the depositing address of every pending deposit that is not its own. Deposits are public and wait at least a day, so there is always time. The published rule then says those deposits are refused, and the honest two thirds, who run the deterministic program, compute and reveal exactly that batch themselves. The flaggers reveal the same batch as everyone else, so they collect no misses and can never be evicted for it. Flags expire after 14 epochs and are re-posted. Anyone can then push the refused deposits back with `refund`.

Cost. With 15 honest screeners the bloc needs 8 seats, about 5.6 ETH or $12,000 of resalable capital at the page's 0.70 ETH a seat, plus stakes that are returned. While nine or fewer screeners are active (the launch condition is seven) the quorum is 3, about 2.1 ETH. The address that holds 77 seats meets one third of any registry with fewer than 155 other active screeners and has to buy nothing. A flag is an event, a few thousand gas each if the operator is a batching contract.

Gain. Exactly what the page prices at two thirds or a week-long majority: choosing who may enter, and so who earns the users' 45% and whose deposits dilute the bloc's own rewards. It also allows targeted censorship of one user for the price of three to eight seats.

The page claims the flag rule was fixed in 4.2: "The quorum is a share of the active screeners, flags expire, and a flag reaches only the flagged address, so three seats cannot have an exchange's customers refused" (`#screening`). That closes the spreading of a flag, not this. Note also that "the flagged address" is `msg.sender` of the deposit: a shared deposit helper contract or a smart-account entry point is one address for all of its users.

Fix. Flags are needed for fresh thefts, so keep them but take away their power to exclude: (a) a flag names one deposit id, not an address, and can be used once per deposit; (b) it does not set the refusal bit; it defers coverage of that one deposit for a fixed number of epochs (see M-4 for how a hold has to be represented), after which the deposit is judged by lists alone; (c) the quorum for anything that refuses outright is a majority of the snapshot, the same threshold the page already discloses as controlling the registry. State in the risks table that the cost of refusing is the flag quorum.

### M-3. A bloc of just over one third can hold the registry to one accepted batch a week for ever, at no cost; the eviction rules do not bound a blockade to "about a week" (high)

Text. `[#screening]`: "A group that reveals something other than the majority's batch for seven days running can be removed by anyone, so a third of the screeners can delay deposits for about a week and no longer." `[#b-contracts]` Registry: "evict(seatId) | Anyone | Removes a screener when the seat has changed hands, when it has not revealed for 7 epochs counted from the later of its last reveal and its first active epoch, or when it has 7 consecutive misses." "What a miss is. ... A screener that revealed something else in such an epoch has one more miss, and a screener that revealed the majority hash has none." "Pay. ... An epoch with no accepted batch pays nobody and its fees stay in the pot." and "a day without a batch delays its share and does not destroy it."

Mechanism A (free, permanent, works under every reading). A bloc with more than one third of the snapshot commits the honest batch every day (anyone can compute it) and simply does not reveal on six days out of seven. Not revealing is not a miss, and the silence rule needs seven epochs. On the seventh day it reveals, the batch is accepted, and its counters are clean. The registry therefore accepts one batch every seven days for as long as the bloc likes. Deposits wait up to about nine days instead of two. The bloc loses nothing: the six days of fees "stay in the pot" and are paid out on the seventh day to everyone who revealed, the bloc included, and the token share is "delayed", not lost. A six-day cycle also stays clear of the rule that sends the screeners' 40% to stakers after seven epochs without a batch. The honest screeners pay gas for six useless commits and reveals each week.

Mechanism B (total blockade, longer than a week). The bloc reveals junk on day 1, stays silent on days 2 to 7, reveals junk on day 8, and so on. It never goes seven epochs without a reveal. Its misses are not "seven days running" in any ordinary sense. The page does not say what a silent epoch does to the count of "consecutive misses" (see guess list). The registry settles a screener's record only at its next commit ("Settles the screener's previous reveal"), so the natural implementation skips silent epochs: the bloc then reaches seven misses after 43 days, not 7. If a builder reads "consecutive" as consecutive epochs and resets the count on a silent epoch, the bloc is never evictable and the blockade is permanent, which is the fault revision 4.2 says it closed.

Mechanism C (rotation). `[#b-contracts]`: "Kept apart from the seat, so a seat that changes hands can be registered again at once." An evicted seat can be re-registered at once and counts again 30 epochs later. Six sets of 8 seats (48 seats, about 34 ETH or $72,000 of resalable capital with 15 honest screeners), staggered a week apart, keep a blocking third active all the time. Each set is silent for its seven days, is evicted, re-registers and returns 30 epochs later.

Cost and gain. A: nothing but gas. B: 8 seats, about $12,000 resalable. C: about $72,000 resalable. Gain: the page's own, "blockers already inside the pool keep the rewards to themselves", plus the ability to sell an end to the blockade.

Fix. (1) Count failures to reveal the accepted or majority hash in a window, not in a row: a screener is evictable when it has failed to reveal the epoch's majority hash in 7 of the last 14 epochs in which a majority hash was recorded, where silence counts as a failure. One counter that goes up by one on a failure and down by one on a success, floored at zero, evictable at 7, does this in one storage field. (2) Do not let the pot pay for blocked days to those who blocked: pay each accepted epoch only the fees received since the previous epoch ended, and send a blocked epoch's fees to stakers. (3) Give a re-registered seat a longer wait after an eviction for cause (for example 90 epochs), so rotation needs far more seats. (4) Replace "about a week and no longer" with what the rules actually bound.

### M-4. A "hold" cannot be expressed in a batch, and "anyone can refund a refused deposit" turns every temporary refusal into a permanent one (ambiguity, high; the two natural readings are both exploitable)

Text. `[#screening]` criteria: `{ "hold": "amount(deposit) > 10 ETH-equivalent && firstFunded(depositor) < 72h", "epochs": 3 }` and `{ "deny": "hopsFromListed(depositor) <= 2 && age(deposit) < 7d" }`. `[#b-contracts]` Batch: only `upTo` and `words` ("refusal bitmap"). `[#b-contracts]` Pool: "refund(id, record) | The depositor at any time before activation. Anyone, while the registry marks the deposit refused". `[#b-states]`: "Refused --> Covered: a later batch clears it". `[#risks]` lists "longer holds for large and freshly funded deposits" as a mitigation twice.

Mechanism. A batch can say two things about a deposit: it is at or below `upTo`, and its bit is set or clear. The page never says which of these a hold uses.

Reading 1, hold = refusal bit for three epochs. Then the deposit is "refused" on-chain and anyone can call `refund` on it. A griefer (or a bot run by people already inside the pool) bounces every held deposit as soon as the batch is applied. The depositor has to deposit again, the new deposit is a new deposit from the same freshly funded address, and it is held again. Large fresh deposits never get in. The same happens to the age-limited deny rule: a deposit refused "while younger than 7 days" is bounced on day 2, re-deposited, and is again younger than 7 days. A rule written as a seven-day delay becomes a permanent ban for anyone a third party cares to bounce. Cost to the griefer: one `refund` call, about 50,000 gas, about $0.02 at 0.2 gwei.

Reading 2, hold = keep `upTo` below the held deposit. `upTo` is one high-water mark, so every later deposit waits too. An attacker deposits 10.01 ETH-equivalent from a fresh address once a day and refunds each deposit after two days. Coverage never gets past his newest held deposit for three epochs at a time, so with one deposit a day nothing behind it is ever activated. Cost: about 20 ETH-equivalent of refundable capital in rotation and two cheap transactions a day. This is a permanent halt of activation by one actor with no seat.

Reading 3, hold = not representable, so the node ignores it. Then the mitigation in the risks table does not exist.

Fix. Give the registry two bit-planes per deposit: `denied` (third parties may refund) and `held` (not approved, not refundable by third parties, clears by itself). `apply` writes both from the batch, and `isRefused` reads only `denied`. Or simpler: drop the third-party refund for anything but a deposit that has been refused in N consecutive applied batches. Write in the criteria schema which rules produce which state, and restrict third-party refunds to rules that cannot expire.

### M-5. `isRefused` has no coverage or age condition, so one faulty batch can let anyone bounce deposits that have not been screened (low to medium)

Text. `[#b-contracts]`: "isRefused(id) | View, called by the pool | The deposit's bit is set". Compare "isApproved(id, depositTime) | ... id ≤ screenedThrough, the deposit's bit is clear, and depositTime + 1 day ≤ dataTime". `[#review2]`: "No batch, however it was produced, can approve a deposit made after that time."

Mechanism. Revision 4.2 protected approval against a faulty batch and left refusal open. A batch may set bits in any 96 words, including words for deposit ids that do not exist yet. One faulty or hostile accepted batch can pre-set 96 × 256 = 24,576 future ids. Every deposit that then takes one of those ids is refundable by anyone from its first block, before any screener has looked at it. Honest later batches clear the bits only as they reach those deposits, at least a day after each is made, so for 24,576 deposits a griefer can bounce each one during its first day.

Fix. `isRefused(id, depositTime)` returns true only if the bit is set and `id ≤ screenedThrough` and `depositTime + 1 day ≤ dataTime`. The pool already has the deposit time in the record.

### M-6. The example hop rule lets any listed address exclude other people by sending them dust (medium; a criteria fault, made worse by M-4)

Text. `[#screening]`: `{ "deny": "hopsFromListed(depositor) <= 2 && age(deposit) < 7d" }` with `"flows": "ETH transfers and Transfer logs of the listed assets, finalized blocks only"`. `[#review2]`: "The example criteria counted three flags and spread them two hops. Flagging an exchange's wallet would have refused its customers" (fixed for flags only).

Mechanism. The people who control listed addresses are the adversaries the rule is aimed at. One of them sends 1 wei, or one unit of USDT, to an exchange hot wallet, a bridge, or directly to a victim. The victim is now one hop from a listed address, and everyone the exchange pays is two hops. Their deposits are refused for the first seven days, and under M-4 can be bounced for ever. The rule has no minimum amount, no direction or proportion test and no exemption for contracts. Cost: one transfer per target.

Fix. This is in the criteria file, not in a contract, so it can be repaired later, but the page presents this file as version 3 of the working rule. Define a hop with a minimum share of the depositor's funding, exclude dust, and never let a hop-based rule set the bit that third parties can act on.

### M-7. The 96-word rule lets `screenedThrough` fall far back and stall every activation (medium)

Text. `[#b-contracts]`: "apply(batch) | ... at most 96 words in ascending order. Sets dataTime to the start of that epoch, sets screenedThrough to the batch's upTo, and overwrites the listed bitmap words" and "If more than 96 words differ, the batch lists the first 96 and lowers upTo to just below the first deposit of the first word left out." `[#b-contracts]` Batch: "the full refusal bitmap of each word that differs from the registry's at the epoch's start".

Mechanism. The page does not say that the program stops evaluating a deposit once it is activated or refunded, and its rules are written per depositor ("listed(depositor)", "flagged(depositor)"). So when one address that appears in many words changes status, every one of those words changes at once. If more than 96 change, `upTo` drops to just below the 97th changed word, which can be years back. Every pending deposit above that point loses coverage (`id ≤ screenedThrough` fails) and stays unactivatable until the backlog is worked through at 96 words a day. With a depositing address present in W words the stall is about W / 96 days, and it happens twice: once when the status is set, and again when a flag expires after 14 epochs.

Who can trigger it. (a) The flag quorum (M-2) against a busy depositing address such as a wallet's deposit helper or an exchange: after one million deposits (3,906 words) that is about 41 days of no activations per flip. (b) An outside listing of a busy address by OFAC or the oracle, with nobody attacking. (c) An attacker's own address that has placed one minimum deposit in each word over time and then becomes refused.

The screeners who cause (a) reveal the honest batch every day and collect no misses. While the total number of deposits is under 24,576 (96 words) nothing happens, so the canary and the first months will not show it. The fork test "a batch with more than 96 changed words" (`#b-deploy`) tests that the rule runs, not what it does to users.

Fix. (1) State that a deposit that is activated or refunded is never evaluated again and its bit is never listed. (2) Do not lower `upTo` below the previous `screenedThrough`. Instead let a batch carry a hash chain of several 96-word chunks, applied in order by anyone, with `isApproved` false for the affected range until the last chunk is in. That keeps one batch a day whatever its size.

### M-8. The buyback step can be made a few hundred wei, for gas only, whenever the price sits at the bottom of the range; and below the start a sandwich does pay (medium)

Text. `[#b-contracts]` FeeDistributor: "buyback() | Anyone, at most once per 12 hours | Swaps IMD for the token in the locked pool. The amount is the smaller of buybackImd and half of the pool's fee rate times the locked position's virtual IMD reserve, which is the position's own liquidity times the pool's current square-root price." Invariant: "A buyback step never exceeds half of the pool's fee rate times the locked position's virtual IMD reserve, and at most one step runs per 12 hours, so the IMD spent in a day never exceeds the size at which a sandwich starts to pay." `[#decisions]`: "cannot be sandwiched at a profit". `[#sim]`: "the month in which the pool's IMD runs out and the price is back at the start".

Part A, the step can be shrunk to nothing. "The pool's current square-root price" is the pool's `slot0` value, and anyone can move it down for free when the locked position holds no IMD. That is the state at launch and the state after every full sell-off, which the page's own tables put between month 2 and month 14 in most rows. Checked in the local v3 source (`UniswapV3Pool.swap`): with the price exactly at the position's lower tick, a token-in swap of 1 wei crosses that tick at zero cost, liquidity becomes zero, and the loop slides the price to the caller's limit with no tokens moving. The attacker's contract does this, then calls `buyback()` in the same transaction. At the minimum v3 price the formula gives about 469 wei of IMD for a one-billion supply (my calculation: 0.005 × L × MIN_SQRT_RATIO / 2^96), against a normal step of 60 IMD. The step runs, spends dust, and the 12-hour timer restarts. Repeating this twice a day costs two small transactions, well under $1 a day, and stops the 20% from ever reaching the market while the price is at the floor. `buybackImd` just grows. When the position does hold IMD, the same trick costs a 2% round trip on that IMD, so it is free only at the floor, which is when the buyback matters most.

Part B, the bound does not hold below the start. The formula uses the locked position's liquidity even when the price is under the position's range and that liquidity is not in play. If the market trades below the start on thin outside liquidity, a 60 IMD step is large against what is really in range. I extended the page's script to this case (`sandwich_ext.py` in my scratch folder, same swap arithmetic): with outside liquidity equal to 5% of the locked liquidity and the price at 0.64 of the start, the best sandwich takes 16 IMD of a 60 IMD step (27%); with 2% outside liquidity and the price at 0.36 of the start it takes 33 IMD (54%). The invariant quoted above is false in that state.

What holds. With the price inside the locked range I could not break the bound. I re-ran `buyback_sandwich_bound.py` and it reproduces `sandwich_results.md`. The script's model fixes the step before the attacker moves, while the contract sizes the step from the price after the attacker's first trade. I tested that difference: at half the product the best profit is still negative for any size of push; at one and a half times the product the profit grows without limit as the push grows, so the margin of two is doing real work and must not be relaxed. Extra in-range liquidity from third parties makes a sandwich less profitable, not more, because it dilutes the step's price impact and the attacker's fee rebate equally.

Fix. Size the step from `max(current sqrt price, starting sqrt price)`, so a parked price cannot shrink it, and do not restart the timer when less than the intended amount was spent. State in the risks table that while the market is under the starting price each step pays the starting price or more and the difference goes to whoever sells into it; that is inherent in buying from a position that starts at the starting price.

### M-9. Every Uniswap formula on the page assumes the new token sorts below IMD, and the launch makes that a coin toss (ambiguity, high: one reading bricks the launch, another removes the buyback bound)

Text. `[#b-contracts]`: "adds what is left of the 25% as one position from the starting price to the top of the tick range", "If someone in the same block has already created it at a higher price", and "the locked position's virtual IMD reserve, which is the position's own liquidity times the pool's current square-root price". `[#b-contracts]`: "a salt taken from the block's prevrandao and the caller".

Mechanism. In Uniswap v3 the pool's price is token1 per token0, with the two tokens ordered by address (`UniswapV3Factory.createPool`, line 41 of the local source). The token's address is now random, so which of the two it is is decided in the launch block. If the token is token0, the page's wording is right. If it is token1: a tokens-only position lies below the current price, from the bottom of the tick range up to the starting tick, so "from the starting price to the top" would need IMD the contract does not have and the mint reverts; "a higher price" in pool terms is a lower token price; and the virtual IMD reserve is liquidity divided by the square-root price, not multiplied. Multiplying in that case gives the virtual token reserve, about 250 million tokens' worth of units read as IMD, so the cap never binds and each buyback spends the whole balance in one sandwichable swap.

IMD's address is not on the page. If it begins with zeros, like the seat contract at 0x0000ec93…, the token will almost always be token1, the case the page does not describe. The canary draws its own random address, so it can pass on one ordering while mainnet gets the other. The caller can also choose the ordering by choosing the calling contract's address.

Fix. Decide the ordering in the build from IMD's actual address, write every formula for that one ordering, and enforce it in the salt loop proposed in M-1 (skip candidates that sort the wrong way). The canary then exercises the same path as mainnet.

### M-10. Staker streams "scaled by 10^18" lose the stablecoin income to rounding (high if built as written)

Text. `[#b-contracts]` FeeDistributor: "stream[asset] | Rate, end time, last update and reward per staked token, scaled by 10^18, for each asset's seven-day stream" and "stake(amount), unstake(amount), claim(assets) | Anyone | Standard reward-per-token accounting, one stream per asset." `[#parameters]`: "Token supply ... One billion is used in examples; any number up to about 79 billion fits". `[#b-contracts]` Token: "18 decimals". `[#verified]`: USDT "has 6 decimals".

Mechanism. Standard accounting adds `elapsed × rate × 10^18 / totalStaked` to the per-token figure at every update and then moves "last update" forward, so whatever rounds away is gone. `totalStaked` is in the token's base units: with one billion tokens and 5% staked it is 5 × 10^25. For a 6-decimal asset the numerator is tiny. In the first cap step the page's own table gives $377 of fees a month; if 60% is stablecoin and stakers get 40%, that is about 35 base units a second, and the increment is 35 × 10^18 / (5 × 10^25) = 7 × 10^-7 per second. It reaches 1 only after about 16 days without any update, and a sweep alone updates the stream once a day. So the per-token figure never moves and stakers receive no USDT or USDC at all. At Railgun-scale fees (say $50,000 a month of stablecoin to stakers, 10% of supply staked) the increment is about 0.002 per block, so anyone who touches the stream more often than every 86 minutes, by accident or to grief, zeroes it. The same truncation applies to "Rate" if it is stored as base units per second: a minimum sweep of 1 USDT sends 0.4 USDT, which is 400,000 units over 604,800 seconds, a rate of zero. The money stays in the distributor for ever. Stablecoins are, by the page's own evidence, more than half of what people shield.

Fix. Scale the per-token figure by 10^36 (it still fits: 10^12 units × 10^36 is far below 2^256), store the rate scaled as well, and advance "last update" only by the time actually paid for. Add a test with a 6-decimal asset, the full supply staked and a minimum-size sweep, and a canary line that checks the amount a staker receives, not only that `claim` was called.
